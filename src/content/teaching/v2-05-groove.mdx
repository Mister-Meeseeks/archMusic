## The question

Some music makes you move almost involuntarily: a head nod, a tapping foot, an urge to get up. Other music, often just as "good," doesn't. Music psychologists call this **groove** and define it narrowly as *the pleasurable urge to move to music*. Listeners agree on it to a striking degree. In one large study, people with very different musical backgrounds largely agreed on which tracks grooved and which didn't (Janata, Tomic & Haberman, 2012).

Musicians have a theory about where groove comes from, usually summed up as **feel**. Great drummers play a little behind the beat, or a little ahead of it. Jazz swings. Drum machines are "stiff" because they're too perfect. On this view, groove lives in tiny departures from metronomic time.

Hold onto that theory, because this chapter tests it. And keep one fact in mind while you do: some of the most danceable records ever made run on machines with perfect timing.

We'll take groove apart in four layers:

1. **The pattern:** which positions in the bar get notes.
2. **Swing:** how the grid itself is bent.
3. **Microtiming:** how individual instruments lean against the grid.
4. **The body:** why any of this makes you move.

## Step 1: The grid

First, the instruments. A standard **drum kit** has four main voices for our purposes:

- The **kick** (bass drum): the low thump, played with a foot pedal.
- The **snare**: the sharp crack, usually on the backbeat (beats 2 and 4).
- The **hi-hat**: a pair of small cymbals that ticks out steady time.
- The **ride**: a large cymbal for longer, ringing time-keeping patterns, the heart of jazz drumming.

Next, the grid. A **drum machine** or **step sequencer** divides one bar of 4/4 into sixteen equal steps, four per beat. Each step is a sixteenth note, counted "**1** e & a **2** e & a **3** e & a **4** e & a." The lab below works the same way.

## Step 2: Syncopation and the sweet spot

**Syncopation** means putting notes on weak positions in the bar and leaving the strong positions empty, so the rhythm pulls against the beat. (Chapter 2.3 covers it properly; here we need a way to measure it.)

### Measuring syncopation

The standard approach, from Christopher Longuet-Higgins and C. S. Lee (1984), starts by giving every position in the bar a **metrical weight**:

| Position | Weight |
|---|---|
| Downbeat (beat 1) | 0 |
| Beat 3 (the half bar) | −1 |
| Beats 2 and 4 | −2 |
| Eighth-note offbeats (the "&"s) | −3 |
| Sixteenth-note positions (the "e"s and "a"s) | −4 |

A **syncopation** happens when a note on a weak position is followed by *silence* on a stronger one. Its size is the difference in weight. A kick on the "a" of beat 4 (weight −4) followed by silence on the next downbeat (weight 0) scores 0 − (−4) = **4**. Add up every syncopation in the pattern and you have its score.

The labs use a simplified version of this, applied to the kick and snare together. The hi-hat stays steady in every pattern. Witek and colleagues used a more elaborate version that accounts for how different drums interact.

### The finding

In 2014, Maria Witek and colleagues ran an online study using funk drum breaks with different amounts of syncopation. Participants rated how much each break made them want to move and how much pleasure it gave them. The result was an **inverted U**:

- Breaks with **little** syncopation scored low.
- Breaks with **a lot** of syncopation also scored low.
- Breaks with **medium** syncopation scored highest on both wanting to move and pleasure.

The effect was strongest in people who enjoy dancing. Before reading why, try it on yourself.

### Why a sweet spot?

The leading explanation comes from **predictive processing**, the idea that the brain constantly predicts what comes next and learns from the mismatch. Peter Vuust and Maria Witek (2014) applied it to groove:

- The meter is a **prediction**. Once you've locked onto a beat, you expect notes in the strong positions.
- A syncopation is a **prediction error**: a note where you didn't expect one, and silence where you did.
- With **too little** syncopation, nothing ever surprises you, so there's nothing to engage with.
- With **too much**, you lose the beat altogether. With no meter left there's nothing for the rhythm to deviate *from*, and the patterns turn into noise.
- At **medium** syncopation, the beat survives but is constantly challenged. Witek (2017) argues that your body steps in to "fill in" the missing beats, which is part of why you move.

That explanation is a good fit, but treat it as a well-supported theory rather than settled fact.

One more wrinkle: rhythm isn't the only ingredient. Matthews and colleagues (2019) found that **harmonic complexity** (how complex the chords are) also affects groove ratings, and that it interacts with rhythmic complexity.

## Step 3: Swing, or bending the grid

So far every pattern has used straight sixteenths: four equal steps per beat. **Swing** changes that. Notes are played in long-short pairs: the first note of each pair is stretched and the second is squeezed.

There are two common ways to describe how much swing:

- **Swing percentage**: the share of the pair taken by the first note. 50% is straight. 67% is **triplet swing**: the first note takes two-thirds of the pair, as if the beat were divided into triplets and the middle one left out. 75% is a hard, dotted feel.
- **Swing ratio**: long to short. 50% is 1:1, 67% is 2:1, and 75% is 3:1.

What gets swung depends on the style. Jazz swings **eighth notes**. Funk, hip-hop, and house swing **sixteenth notes**. Drum machines adopted swing as a single knob. Roger Linn's drum machines, and later the Akai MPC he co-designed, offered swing settings from 50% to 75%. The MPC's swing became part of the sound of hip-hop.

<Callout kind="try">
1. Play the **jazz ride pattern** and drag **Swing** from 50% up to 75%. Watch the timing map: the ride's offbeat notes (the "&"s) slide later, toward the next beat. At 50% the pattern sounds like a march. Somewhere around 60–67% it starts to swing.
2. Raise the tempo to 200 BPM and try 67% again. At fast tempos, heavy swing starts to sound lumpy. Bring it down toward 55–60% and it flows again.
3. Still on the jazz pattern, switch **Swing what** to **sixteenth notes** and move the slider. The *sound* doesn't change. The orange arrows on the timing map still move, but they point at empty slots: every ride note is on a beat or an "&", and sixteenth swing moves only the "e"s and "a"s in between. Swing only affects notes on the positions it bends.
4. Switch to the **half-time shuffle**. Here the *sixteenths* are swung at a triplet feel, and the snare lands only on beat 3, so the groove feels half as fast as the tempo. Set the swing to 50% and the shuffle collapses into a stiff straight-sixteenth beat.
</Callout>

That second experiment points to a real finding. When Anders Friberg and Andreas Sundström (2002) measured jazz drummers' ride cymbal patterns, the swing ratio **changed with tempo**. It was as high as about **3.5:1** at slow tempos and approached **1:1** (even) at fast tempos. The textbook 2:1 "triplet feel" appeared only around one particular tempo. Soloists also swung less than the drummers behind them, especially at slow tempos. So "swing = triplets" is a convenient way of writing it down, not a description of what players actually do.

<Callout kind="check">
Why does a funk pattern full of sixteenth notes change a lot under sixteenth swing, while a rock beat with only eighth-note hi-hats doesn't change at all?

<Answer>
Sixteenth swing moves only the in-between positions (the "e"s and "a"s). A rock beat with eighth-note hi-hats has nothing there, so there's nothing to move. To swing it you'd have to swing the eighth notes instead, which moves the "&"s. A pattern's *subdivision* determines which kind of swing it can have.
</Answer>

## Step 4: Microtiming, or leaning on the beat

Swing bends the whole grid evenly. **Microtiming** is subtler: small, consistent timing offsets for particular instruments, typically somewhere around 10 to 40 milliseconds. A snare played consistently a little late makes a groove sound **laid back**. A hi-hat played a little early makes it sound **pushing**, urgent, on top of the beat. These offsets are small enough that most listeners don't hear a mistake. They hear a *character*.

<GrooveLab preset="laidback" presets={['laidback', 'rock']} controls={['presets', 'tempo', 'offsets', 'humanize']} title="Microtiming" />

<Callout kind="try">
1. Play the **laid-back neo-soul** pattern. The snare is set 28 ms late and the kick 8 ms early. Find the snare dots on the timing map: they sit just *after* the grid lines for beats 2 and 4.
2. Drag the snare back to **on the grid** and listen. It isn't worse, exactly, but it's more upright and less lazy. Now try 40 ms late. At some point "laid back" turns into "sloppy." Where does it cross over for you?
3. Switch to the **basic rock beat** and push the hi-hat 15 ms early. Does it sound more urgent?
4. Set **Humanize** to around 10 ms. That adds random scatter to every hit, like a real (imperfect) drummer. Compare it with consistent offsets: random scatter tends to sound sloppy, while consistent offsets sound like a choice.
</Callout>

That last contrast matters. Microtiming in the musicians' sense is *systematic*. It's a consistent relationship between parts, not random error.

Two recordings are the touchstones for this kind of feel:

**J Dilla.** The Detroit producer programmed drums on an MPC without snapping everything to the grid, and set different instruments to different degrees of swing. Kicks and snares drift against the hi-hat, sometimes early, sometimes late. Dan Charnas's book *Dilla Time* (2022) describes the result as straight and swung feels happening at once, and traces its influence through hip-hop, neo-soul, and jazz.

<ListeningCue track="slum-village-fall-in-love" start="0:00" end="0:45">
Listen to the kick and snare against the hi-hat. They don't sit where a quantized drum machine would put them, yet the groove is completely solid. Try nodding along and notice where your head lands.
</ListeningCue>

**D'Angelo's *Voodoo*** (2000). Questlove on drums and Pino Palladino on bass play famously far behind the beat, in a deliberate imitation of Dilla's programming. The musicologist Anne Danielsen analyzed "Left & Right" and argued that its parts imply several slightly different versions of where the beat is, all at once. On her account the beat isn't a point in time but a zone with *width*, which she calls a **beat bin**.

## Step 5: Does feel make the groove? The experiments

Now back to the musicians' theory. Its best-known academic form is Charles Keil's idea of **participatory discrepancies** (1987): music grooves *because* musicians are slightly out of sync with each other. The small frictions between parts are what draw listeners in.

It's an appealing idea, and it matches how many players describe their experience. It has also been tested quite directly, by taking grooves and varying their microtiming:

- **Davies, Madison, Silva & Gouyon (2013)** added microtiming deviations to drum and bass grooves. Groove ratings did *not* go up, and larger deviations brought them down.
- **Frühauf, Kopiez & Platz (2013)** shifted snare and kick timing in a rock pattern. The perfectly quantized version was rated best, and early shifts were rated worse than late ones.
- **Senn, Kilchenmann, von Georgi & Bullerjahn (2016)** used real recordings of expert swing and funk rhythm sections. The original microtiming and a fully quantized version were rated *equally* groovy. Exaggerated microtiming was rated lower.
- **Madison et al. (2011)** found that groove ratings across many tracks went with **event density, beat salience, and syncopation**, not with the amount of microtiming.

So the evidence doesn't support the strong version of the theory. Perfectly quantized music grooves at least as well, and the urge to move seems to come mainly from **the pattern**: syncopation, how busy the rhythm is, and how clearly the beat comes through.

That doesn't make microtiming irrelevant:

- These experiments mostly measure listeners' ratings of short excerpts. Feel may matter more for *players*, and for long-term engagement, than a rating scale can capture.
- Microtiming clearly shapes **style and character**. Laid-back versus pushing, a Dilla beat versus a drum machine: these sound different even if they groove equally.
- Expert listeners may respond differently. Kilchenmann and Senn (2015) found that microtiming affected how musically trained listeners *moved*, even when it didn't change their ratings much.

## Step 6: Why the body?

The final layer is the most basic: why should rhythm make us *move* at all?

Part of the answer is **entrainment**: the tendency of a rhythmic system to lock its timing to an external rhythm. Two pendulum clocks on the same wall drift into sync. You start tapping along without deciding to. Humans are unusually good at this. We can find a beat in complex music and synchronize to it, across a wide range of tempos, which few other animals can do.

Brain imaging shows how close the connection between hearing rhythm and moving really is. Listening to rhythms with a clear beat activates motor areas of the brain (including the basal ganglia and supplementary motor area) even when people are lying perfectly still (Grahn & Brett, 2007). Hearing a beat is, in part, preparing to move to it.

Put that together with step 2 and you have a plausible story. A groove sets up a beat your motor system locks onto, then syncopation leaves gaps in it, and your body fills them. Chapter 2.7 covers entrainment and synchronization in depth.

## The layers together

| Layer | What it is | What the evidence says |
|---|---|---|
| **Pattern** | Which positions get notes: syncopation, density, ghost notes | The main driver of groove. Medium syncopation is the sweet spot. |
| **Swing** | Bending the grid into long-short pairs | Defines style. Real players' swing ratios change with tempo. |
| **Microtiming** | Consistent offsets between instruments | Shapes feel and character. Doesn't raise groove ratings in experiments. |
| **Body** | Entrainment and motor involvement | Why rhythm makes us move at all. |

This mirrors chapter 1.5. There, "dissonance" turned out to be several different things stacked on top of each other: physics, perception, learned preference. Here, "groove" splits the same way, and the folk explanation (feel) turns out to describe a real layer, just not the one that does most of the work.
