## The question

Play a C major chord on a piano. Then play C, C♯, and D together. The first sounds smooth and settled; the second grinds. Almost anyone raised on Western music hears the difference instantly, so it's easy to assume it's simply a fact about the notes.

People have argued about *why* for roughly 2,500 years. Three main answers have come out of that argument:

1. **Ratios (Pythagoras, ~500 BCE).** An interval is consonant when its two frequencies form a simple whole-number ratio: 2:1, 3:2, 4:3. The simpler the ratio, the sweeter the sound.
2. **Roughness (Hermann von Helmholtz, 1863).** Dissonance is a sensation. When frequencies sit close together they interfere and produce a rapid fluttering that the ear finds harsh. Consonance is simply the absence of that roughness.
3. **Culture.** What counts as consonant is learned. Medieval European theorists counted thirds as imperfect. Jazz musicians treat a ninth as a color. Some vocal traditions sing in close seconds on purpose.

All three turn out to be partly right. The interesting part is how they fit together. By the end of this chapter you should be able to say exactly *which part* of "dissonance" each one explains.

Keep two terms separate from the start:

- **Sensory dissonance (roughness):** a physical and physiological effect that can be modeled and measured.
- **Consonance preference:** whether listeners *like* certain combinations. This is partly learned.

A third meaning, **functional dissonance**, shows up at the end.

## Step 1: Two pure tones

Start as simply as possible, with two **sine waves**. A sine wave is a pure tone: one frequency, no harmonics. Pure tones are rare in nature (tuning forks, flutes, and whistling come close), but they're the atoms of sound. Every sound is a sum of them, which is the subject of chapter 1.2.

<BeatingLab />

<Callout kind="try">
Start the sound and slowly drag the difference up from zero. You should hear four stages:

1. **0 Hz:** one steady tone.
2. **About 1–12 Hz:** one tone whose loudness pulses, *wah-wah-wah*. These are **beats**, and the number of pulses per second equals the frequency difference.
3. **About 15–60 Hz:** the pulsing is too fast to follow and turns into a buzzy, grainy texture. This is **roughness**.
4. **Beyond that:** the roughness fades and you hear two separate, smooth notes.

Then switch the lower tone to 110 Hz and to 880 Hz and repeat. Where does the roughness peak move? Where does the shaded band move?
</Callout>

### Why beats happen

Add two waves of slightly different frequency and they drift in and out of phase. Where their peaks line up they reinforce each other, which is loud. Half a cycle of drift later, peaks meet troughs and they cancel, which is quiet. They come back into line once per *unit of frequency difference*: 440 Hz plus 443 Hz pulses three times per second. You can see this in the top plot, where the combined wave sits inside an envelope that swells and shrinks.

This part is plain physics; water waves do the same thing. Musicians use it constantly. Piano tuners and guitarists adjust a string until the beating against a reference slows down and stops.

### Why roughness happens: the critical band

Beating turning into roughness, and roughness turning into two clear notes, is not physics. It's your **cochlea**.

The inner ear works roughly like a bank of overlapping filters. Along the basilar membrane, each spot responds most strongly to one frequency: high frequencies near one end, low frequencies near the other. Each spot also responds to a *range* of frequencies around its best one. That range is the **critical band**.

- When two tones fall within one critical band, they excite the same stretch of membrane and the ear can't separate them. It hears their interaction instead: beats when the difference is slow, roughness when it's fast.
- When the tones are far enough apart to excite separate regions, the ear hears two independent tones. The interaction drops out of perception, even though the combined waveform is still beating physically.

In 1965 Reinier Plomp and Willem Levelt had listeners rate pairs of pure tones. Dissonance peaked when the tones were about **a quarter of a critical band apart** and had mostly gone by about one full critical band. That curve is the bottom plot in the lab. The labs throughout this chapter use William Sethares's mathematical fit of it.

The critical band is **not a fixed musical interval.** Around <Term id="middle-c">middle C</Term> it's very roughly a <Term id="interval">minor third</Term> wide. An octave lower it's closer to a major third, and two octaves lower it's nearly a fifth. In Hz the band shrinks as you go down, but more slowly than the notes themselves get closer together. That fact matters a great deal in step 4.

<Callout kind="check">
Two pure tones at 1000 Hz and 1004 Hz: what do you hear? What about 1000 Hz and 1500 Hz?

<Answer>
1000 + 1004 Hz: one tone, beating four times per second. 1000 + 1500 Hz: two smooth, separate tones a perfect fifth apart. They're far more than a critical band apart, so they don't interact perceptually.
</Answer>
</Callout>

## Step 2: The surprise with pure tones

Here is the first real surprise. With **pure tones**, every interval wider than about a critical band is *equally smooth*. The perfect fifth (3:2) has no special status, and neither does the octave. A slightly out-of-tune fifth sounds just as good as a perfect one.

<IntervalExplorer initialTimbre="sine" timbres={['sine']} initialCents={300} />

<Callout kind="try">
Drag the interval from unison up to the octave. The roughness curve has one hump near the bottom and is flat after that: no dips at the fifth, the fourth, or anywhere else. Play a major seventh (M7) and then an octave. With sine tones the major seventh sounds surprisingly harmless.
</Callout>

So if the Pythagorean "simple ratios" story were about frequencies alone, it would be wrong. Something else must be going on.

## Step 3: Real tones have harmonics

The something else is that **real instruments don't produce sine waves.** A plucked string, a sung vowel, or a trumpet note produces a fundamental frequency *f* and also a stack of **harmonics** (also called overtones or partials) at 2*f*, 3*f*, 4*f*, 5*f*, and so on. This stack is the **harmonic series**. You don't hear the harmonics as separate notes. They fuse into a single pitch and give the sound its color (chapter 1.2 covers this in depth).

So two notes played together aren't two sine waves interacting. They might be twenty, and **every pair** of partials that falls within a critical band adds some roughness.

<IntervalExplorer initialTimbre="harmonic" initialCents={700} />

<Callout kind="try">
1. Start on the perfect fifth (P5) and look at the partials diagram. Several pairs of partials are joined by green lines, meaning they **coincide exactly**. The lower tone's 3rd harmonic is the same frequency as the upper tone's 2nd, its 6th matches the upper tone's 4th, and so on.
2. Drag slowly away from the fifth, just 10–20 <Term id="cents">cents</Term>. The green links turn red. Those partials are now *slightly* apart, so they produce beating. Play it and listen for a slow wobble.
3. Look at the curve. Dips have appeared at the octave, fifth, fourth, major sixth, major third, and minor third, roughly in that order of depth. The minor second, major seventh, and tritone sit on high ground.
4. Drag the **Partials** slider down to 2, then up to 16. The more harmonics a tone has, the more dips there are and the sharper they get.
</Callout>

This is Helmholtz's big insight, made precise by Plomp & Levelt and later by Sethares: **simple ratios are consonant because they make harmonics line up.** At exactly 3:2 the two harmonic series interlock. Many partials coincide, and most of the rest are comfortably far apart. Move off the ratio and you get a crowd of near-misses, each one beating.

The more complex the ratio, the fewer the coincidences and the more the near-misses. A minor second (16:15) has almost no coincidences, and it puts the two fundamentals themselves inside one critical band.

Pythagoras had noticed something real, but for a reason he had no way of knowing. Ratios matter **because instrument tones are harmonic.** That raises an obvious question: what happens when they aren't?

<Callout kind="check">
For a violin, why is a mistuned fifth (3:2 plus 15 cents) rougher than a perfect fifth, when for two sine waves it isn't?

<Answer>
The violin's tones have near-coinciding partials: the lower tone's 3rd harmonic against the upper tone's 2nd, and so on. At exactly 3:2 those pairs coincide and don't beat. Mistuned, they sit a few Hz apart inside one critical band, so they beat and roughen the sound. Sine waves have no upper partials, so there's nothing to mistune: the two fundamentals are far apart either way.
</Answer>
</Callout>

## Step 4: Register: the same chord gets rougher down low

Go back to the interval explorer above. Set it to a major third and drag the **Register** slider down to C2. The dips get shallower and the rough region near the bottom of the curve widens. The same major third that's sweet around middle C turns muddy near the bottom of the piano.

The chord lab below also introduces two more ideas. A chord's **voicing** is how its notes are spread across octaves. A *close* voicing packs them within one octave (C–E–G). An *open* voicing spreads them out, for example C, then G, then the E an octave higher. A **power chord** is just a root, a fifth, and an octave, with no third at all.

The critical band explains it. At C2 a major third is 65 Hz against 82 Hz, a difference of only 17 Hz. That puts the fundamentals almost exactly at the roughness peak for that register (about 19 Hz), and many of the low harmonics land close together as well.

<ChordRoughness initialMidi={60} />

<Callout kind="try">
Play the **close major triad** at C4, then drag the root down to C2 while it's playing and listen to it go muddy. Now compare the **open voicing** (root, fifth, and the third an octave higher) down at C2. Notice also that the **power chord** stays clean even very low.
</Callout>

This is the acoustics behind two pieces of musicians' practical knowledge:

- **Low interval limits.** Orchestration and arranging guides give the lowest pitch at which each interval still sounds clear. Thirds go muddy below roughly an octave under middle C; seconds go muddy much higher. That's why bass parts favor roots, fifths, and octaves and leave the thirds and sevenths to higher instruments.
- **Piano voicings.** Pianists play open fifths and tenths in the left hand and keep the close chord shapes in the right.

## Step 5: Change the timbre, change the consonance

If consonance comes from harmonic partials lining up, then a sound whose partials are **inharmonic** should have *different* consonant intervals. It does, and this is one of the most striking ideas in the chapter.

Bells, gongs, metal bars, and many drums produce partials that are **not** whole-number multiples of the fundamental. Sethares, who wrote the book on this (*Tuning, Timbre, Spectrum, Scale*), showed that you can predict where the consonant intervals of such a sound will fall, and that they move away from the familiar ratios.

<IntervalExplorer initialTimbre="stretched" timbres={['harmonic', 'stretched']} initialCents={1200} />

<Callout kind="try">
This tone's partials are "stretched." Its second partial sits at 2.1 times the fundamental instead of exactly 2, and the higher partials are stretched to match.

1. Play a normal octave (P8, 1200¢). It sounds slightly sour, and the partials diagram is full of red.
2. Drag to the dashed green line, the "stretched octave" at about 1284¢. The roughness dips and the partials line up. The wide, "wrong" octave now sounds *more* consonant than the true one.
3. Move the **Stretch** slider and watch the whole landscape of consonant intervals slide along with it.
</Callout>

Two real-world echoes of this:

**Gamelan.** Javanese and Balinese gamelan orchestras are built mostly from metal: tuned bronze bars and gongs with inharmonic spectra. Their tuning systems, *slendro* and *pelog*, don't line up with Western ratios. Sethares argues that gamelan tunings fit the spectra of gamelan instruments the way Western scales fit harmonic spectra. It's an elegant hypothesis, but it's also contested: gamelan tunings vary a lot from ensemble to ensemble, and cultural preference clearly plays a part.

Balinese gamelan adds a twist. Instruments come in pairs tuned a few Hz apart on purpose, so that together they **beat** and produce a shimmering pulse called *ombak* ("wave"). That's step 1's beating, used as an aesthetic goal rather than treated as a flaw.

<ListeningCue track="gamelan-kebyar">
Listen to the metallophones, especially in the sustained and loud passages. The shimmer, a fast trembling inside the sound, comes from paired instruments beating against each other. Compare it with the BeatingLab set to about 5–8 Hz.
</ListeningCue>

**Distortion and the power chord.** Guitar distortion is another kind of timbre change. It adds many new partials, including sums and differences of the frequencies being played (*intermodulation*). Put a clean major triad through heavy distortion and the third generates a mess of clashing products, so the chord turns to mud. A root and fifth (plus octave) generates products that mostly fall on the same harmonic series, so it stays solid. That's a big part of why distorted rock guitar runs on **power chords**: they're the chords that survive the timbre.

<ListeningCue track="nirvana-teen-spirit" start="0:00" end="0:30">
The opening riff is all power chords: roots and fifths, no thirds. It's played clean first, then slammed with distortion. Now imagine full major and minor triads through that much distortion. The genre's harmonic vocabulary is shaped by its timbre.
</ListeningCue>

## Step 6: Tuning, and how the third became consonant

Tuning is the same principle in another form. A keyboard can't make every interval a perfect ratio. Stack twelve pure fifths (3:2) and you overshoot seven octaves by about a quarter of a semitone, an amount called the **Pythagorean comma**. Every tuning system has to decide where to hide that error, and the choice changes how rough particular intervals sound.

<TuningComparator initialShape="M3" />

<Callout kind="try">
Play the major third in each system in this order: **just**, then **equal**, then **Pythagorean**. The just third is still. The equal-tempered third wobbles gently, about 10 beats per second at middle C. The Pythagorean third buzzes at about 16. Then try the perfect fifth: Pythagorean and just are identical and still, and equal temperament is nearly still.
</Callout>

The history lines up with this. **Medieval European music was theorized in Pythagorean tuning,** which is built entirely from pure fifths. In that tuning fifths and fourths are perfectly smooth, and thirds are rough: the Pythagorean major third (81:64) is about 22 cents wider than the just 5:4. So it's no coincidence that medieval theory treated octaves, fifths, and fourths as the *perfect* consonances and thirds as imperfect at best, or that pieces long ended on open fifths and octaves rather than full triads.

From the fifteenth century on, thirds became central to European polyphony; English music of that period was noted for its sweet thirds and sixths. Tuning systems such as **meantone** narrowed the fifths slightly so that the thirds came out closer to pure. Modern **equal temperament** is a compromise: every key is equally usable, the fifths are almost pure, and the thirds are noticeably wide, by about 14 cents.

Groups that can adjust their tuning freely tend to drift toward just intonation on sustained chords. That includes string quartets, choirs, and above all **barbershop quartets**. When a barbershop chord "locks," the coinciding partials reinforce each other so strongly that listeners report hearing an extra high note ringing above the chord.

<ListeningCue track="barbershop-tag" start="3:20">
Vocal Spectrum were the 2006 international barbershop champions, and this ballad gives them time to let chords lock. Listen to the final sustained chord. When it locks, the sound seems to get brighter and steadier at the same moment. That's what coinciding partials sound like. Some listeners hear a faint extra pitch on top.
</ListeningCue>

## Step 7: Is roughness the whole story?

The roughness model has now explained a lot: beats, the critical band, why simple ratios matter, register, timbre, and tuning. It doesn't explain everything, though, and research over the last fifteen years has complicated the picture in a useful way.

### Harmonicity: a second mechanism

A competing account focuses on **harmonicity**: how closely a chord's combined partials resemble *one* harmonic series. The partials of a just major triad (4:5:6) all fit inside a single harmonic series built on a low fundamental, two octaves below the root. The auditory system evolved to group harmonic partials into single sound sources (voices, for instance), so to it, a chord like this looks almost like one rich tone. A dissonant chord's partials don't fit any single series well.

In 2010 Josh McDermott, Andriana Lehr, and Andrew Oxenham measured how much individual listeners preferred consonance, how much they preferred harmonicity, and how much they disliked roughness. Consonance preference tracked **harmonicity** preference far more closely than it tracked dislike of roughness. Their conclusion was that harmonicity, more than roughness, drives the *preference* for consonant chords, at least among Western listeners.

The two accounts usually make similar predictions, because simple ratios both reduce roughness *and* increase harmonicity. They come apart in edge cases. Current models, such as Peter Harrison and Marcus Pearce's 2020 composite model, combine roughness, harmonicity, *and* familiarity.

### Culture: the Tsimane' experiment

Then there's the cross-cultural evidence.

In 2016 McDermott and colleagues tested the **Tsimane'**, an Indigenous people of the Bolivian Amazon. At the time they had little exposure to Western music, and their own music is mostly sung by one person at a time, without chords. The results:

- Tsimane' listeners **disliked acoustic roughness**, just as Americans did.
- They reacted to non-musical sounds (laughter versus gasps) the same way other groups did.
- But they rated **consonant and dissonant chords as equally pleasant**. Bolivian town and city dwellers showed a small preference for consonance. US listeners showed a large one, largest among musicians.

A 2025 follow-up found that Tsimane' participants who were **more integrated into the global economy and culture** showed a small but significant preference for consonance, and less-integrated participants showed none.

This is the clearest evidence so far that **roughness and consonance are different things**:

- **Disliking roughness** looks broadly human: a sensory fact rooted in the cochlea.
- **Preferring consonant chords** appears to be *learned*, probably from exposure to music built on harmonic chords. That fits the harmonicity account: hearing a lot of chordal music would teach you to value chords that fuse.

<Callout kind="note">
Don't overcorrect to "it's all cultural." The physics and physiology (beating, critical bands, which partials coincide) are universal. What varies is what listeners *do* with those sensations: whether smoothness is valued, and whether roughness counts as a flaw or a color.
</Callout>

### Roughness as a feature

Plenty of traditions use roughness on purpose.

<ListeningCue track="bulgarian-polegnala">
Bulgarian women's choirs sing close harmonies that include sustained **major and minor seconds**, in a bright, forward vocal tone. By Western common-practice rules those intervals are dissonant. Here the clash is the point: a ringing, buzzing intensity. Bosnian *ganga* singing goes even further in the same direction.
</ListeningCue>

<ListeningCue track="stravinsky-augurs" start="0:00" end="0:40">
The famous repeated chord in "Augurs of Spring" stacks an E♭7 chord on top of an F♭ major triad, two chords whose roots are a semitone apart. The strings hammer it percussively. It's roughness used as rhythm and force, and it was shocking in 1913.
</ListeningCue>

<ListeningCue track="ligeti-atmospheres" start="0:00" end="1:30">
Ligeti fills the texture with **tone clusters**: dozens of neighboring notes sounding at once. At that density, individual dissonances stop registering as "wrong notes" and merge into one shimmering, slowly shifting mass. Dissonance has become timbre. Kubrick used this piece in *2001: A Space Odyssey*.
</ListeningCue>

## Step 8: The third kind of dissonance: musical function

One last distinction, which sets up Volume III.

In <Term id="common-practice">common-practice</Term> harmony (the conventions of European art music from roughly Bach to Brahms, which most music theory textbooks teach), "dissonance" often means something else: a note that is **unstable in the style** and expected to <Term id="resolution">resolve</Term>, that is, to move to a stable note. Two examples show how far this can be from roughness:

- In traditional <Term id="counterpoint">counterpoint</Term> (the craft of combining independent melodies), a **perfect fourth** above the bass counts as a *dissonance*, even though acoustically it's one of the smoothest intervals there is (4:3). Its instability is grammatical, not sensory.
- A <Term id="dominant-seventh">**dominant seventh chord**</Term> (G–B–D–F: a major triad with a minor seventh on top) "wants" to resolve to C. Some of that pull is roughness (the B–F tritone), but most of it is *learned expectation*: you've heard that resolution thousands of times. Jazz takes the same chord, adds <Term id="extensions">ninths and thirteenths</Term> (notes stacked even higher above the chord), and lets it sit unresolved, because in that style it's a color rather than a problem.

So "dissonance" names at least three different things:

| Kind | What it is | Where it comes from |
|---|---|---|
| **Sensory (roughness)** | Beating and buzzing between nearby partials | Physics and the cochlea; broadly universal |
| **Consonance preference** | Liking chords whose partials fuse or line up | Harmonicity plus learning; varies by culture |
| **Functional dissonance** | A note that "needs to resolve" within a style | Learned grammar and expectation; style-specific |

That three-level structure is the whole course in miniature: **physics → perception → learned grammar → culture.** Each level constrains the next without fully determining it.
