## The question

Chapter 2.2 argued that a beat is something the listener does: you take a stream of sounds and impose a steady pulse and a hierarchy of strong and weak beats on it. Chapter 2.3 showed rhythms pulling against that structure, with syncopations and accents that land where the meter says they shouldn't.

This chapter goes further. What if the music contains **two** rhythmic frameworks at once, and they disagree? A pianist's right hand plays four even notes while the left hand plays three in the same time. A bell plays a pattern that cuts across the beat the dancers feel. A guitar riff seven steps long repeats over drums that count in eights. Two pianists play the same pattern and one gradually pulls ahead.

These aren't rare effects. Layered, disagreeing rhythms are the normal texture of much West African and Afro-Caribbean music; they're central to jazz, to a lot of progressive rock and metal, and to minimalism; and they turn up in Chopin and Brahms. The interesting questions are what the listener does with them (can you hear two beats at once?) and why so many musical traditions find this kind of disagreement worth building on.

Four terms get used, and often blurred:

- **Polyrhythm:** two pulses at once that divide the same span into different numbers of equal parts (3 against 2).
- **Cross-rhythm:** a rhythm that cuts across the meter the listener holds.
- **Polymeter:** patterns of different lengths on the same pulse, drifting apart and back together.
- **Phasing:** one pattern against a copy of itself, offset in time.

Each gets a section.

## Two pulses at once

### Three against two

Take a span of time, say one second, and divide it two ways at once: one part plays three evenly spaced notes, another plays two. That's a **polyrhythm**, in this case **3 against 2** (written 3:2). The two pulses start together, go their separate ways, and meet again at the start of the next span.

To play or picture both, you need a grid fine enough to hold both pulses. For 3:2 that's six cells: the "three" falls on cells 1, 3, and 5, the "two" on cells 1 and 4. Put them together and you get the **composite rhythm**, the pattern you actually hear when both parts sound: notes on cells 1, 3, 4, and 5, a long-short-short-long figure. Musicians learn it by saying a phrase with the same rhythm: *nice cup of tea*.

</Callout>

The general rule for any ratio n:m: the grid needs lcm(n, m) cells, the least common multiple. When n and m share no common factor (3 and 2, 4 and 3, 5 and 4), that's simply n × m. The composite has n + m − 1 notes per cycle, because the two pulses share only their first note.

### Hearing a polyrhythm

Can a listener hear two beats at once? The research says: mostly not, at least not as two equal beats.

When people are asked to tap along to a polyrhythm, they almost always tap one of the two pulses, treating it as the beat and the other as a rhythm against it. Which one they choose depends on the sound. Stephen Handel and James Oshinsky (1981) found that the choice shifted with tempo, with which pulse was higher in pitch, and with which was louder or accented. At fast tempos, listeners favored the slower pulse, which keeps the beat inside the range where a beat can be felt.

Can trained musicians do better? Ève Poudrier and Bruno Repp (2013) tested whether classical musicians could track two different beats at the same time. They could, under limited conditions, for a simple ratio like 3:2 and with the two pulses clearly separated, but performance fell off as the patterns got more complex. Their interpretation: rather than holding two independent beats, listeners hold *one* metrical framework, a grid fine enough to contain both pulses, and hear the two as different levels or patterns within it. That's what the lab's grid draws.

This connects to chapter 1.4. If the two pulses are far apart in register and timbre, like the lab's high tok and low drum, the ear tends to split them into two **auditory streams**, and it becomes easier to follow either one. If they're in the same register and timbre, they merge into a single stream, and you hear the composite rhythm as one pattern. That's why "nice cup of tea" works as a learning aid: it turns two pulses into one rhythm you can say.

### From polyrhythm to tempo change

A polyrhythm can also be a doorway to a new tempo. If triplets have been running against the beat, a composer can declare that the triplet is now the beat: the music suddenly speeds up by exactly a ratio of 3 to 2, with no break in the pulse. This is called **metric modulation**, a term first applied to the music of Elliott Carter in the 1940s and 1950s. Rock drummers do a casual version all the time when they slide into a half-time or double-time feel (chapter 2.3); metric modulation is the same idea with less obvious ratios.

</Callout>

## Cross-rhythm and the timeline

### A bell that cuts across the beat

A polyrhythm as described above is symmetrical: two pulses of equal standing. **Cross-rhythm** is the more general idea of a rhythm that cuts across the meter the listener holds. The listener keeps the meter; the cross-rhythm pulls against it, persistently, as a texture rather than an occasional syncopation.

The richest cross-rhythmic music is from West and Central Africa and its diaspora. A typical Ewe dance-drumming ensemble from Ghana and Togo layers several parts over a 12-pulse cycle:

- a **bell** playing a fixed pattern of seven strokes in the twelve pulses, repeated unchanged for the whole piece;
- **rattles** or handclaps marking a steady beat;
- **support drums** playing short repeating patterns, each with its own relationship to the bell;
- a **lead drum** improvising, speaking, and signaling changes to the dancers.

The bell's pattern is called a **timeline pattern**: a short, asymmetric pattern that orients every other part, in the way the clave does in Cuban music (chapter 2.2). The most widespread version, on a 12-pulse cycle, is the one in the lab below and in chapter 2.2's 12-pulse bell pattern.

Twelve pulses can be grouped in several ways: four beats of three pulses, three beats of four, six beats of two. The dancers' feet usually move in four beats (every three pulses). Other parts suggest three or six. The bell's strokes fit none of them neatly. The result is a texture where 3 against 2 and 4 against 3 are always present, because they are built into the twelve-pulse cycle itself.

### Inherent patterns

Gerhard Kubik (1962) described a striking effect in East and Central African music for xylophones and lamellophones (instruments like the mbira). When several players interlock fast parts, listeners hear melodies and rhythms that none of the players is playing: notes from different parts that happen to fall close in pitch group into new lines. Kubik called these **inherent patterns**. They are scene analysis (chapter 1.4) at work: the ear regroups notes by pitch across players, so the texture contains more rhythms than the performers play. Musicians in these traditions compose with this effect deliberately.

### A caution about "African polyrhythm"

Western writing about African music has a long habit of treating "African rhythm" as one thing, uniquely complex, and the opposite of European harmony. The Ghanaian music theorist Kofi Agawu (1995, 2003) has criticized this as an invention of outsiders. His points are worth keeping in mind:

- There are hundreds of distinct musical traditions in Africa, not one.
- Much of the "complexity" is in the notation, not in the music as performers experience it. If a transcriber assumes the wrong beat, every part looks syncopated against it.
- Performers usually hear a clear beat, often given by the dance, and the timeline pattern is heard *relative to that beat*, not as a free-floating rhythm.
- Describing African music only in terms of rhythm ignores its melody, harmony, language, and form.

David Locke's detailed studies of Ewe drumming (1982, 1987) take a middle path: they show precisely how each part relates to the bell and the beat, and they treat cross-rhythm as a carefully organized system rather than as exotic complexity.

<Callout kind="check">
Twelve pulses can be heard as four beats of three, three beats of four, or six beats of two. Why does this make the 12-pulse cycle such a good home for polyrhythm?

<Answer>
Because 12 divides evenly by 2, 3, 4, and 6, every pair of those groupings forms a polyrhythm that fits inside one cycle: four beats against three is 4 : 3, six against four is 3 : 2, and so on. Different parts (feet, rattles, drums, bell) can each emphasize a different grouping, and they all realign at the start of every cycle. An 8-pulse or 16-pulse cycle only divides into twos and fours, so it can't hold 3 : 2 without tuplets.
</Answer>
</Callout>

## Loops of different lengths: polymeter

### The same pulse, different cycles

In a polyrhythm, the two parts move at different speeds but share a cycle. **Polymeter** reverses this: both parts step on the same pulse, but they repeat after different numbers of steps. A three-step figure over a four-step bar starts in a different place in each bar. The figure and the bar line up again only after twelve steps, the least common multiple of 3 and 4.

A short figure repeated persistently like this is called an **ostinato** (Italian for "stubborn"). An ostinato that matches the bar repeats in the same place every time. An ostinato of a different length drifts across the bar, landing somewhere new each time it comes round.

<PolyrhythmLab modes={['polymeter']} />

<Callout kind="try">
1. Play **3 against 4**. The wood-block figure is three steps long; the kick and hi-hat mark a four-step bar. Watch the figure's accent (the big circle) move to a different step of the bar each time, until both start together again after 12 steps.
2. Mute the **Bar**. Now the figure alone just sounds like a steady waltz-like pattern. Bring the bar back: the same figure now pulls against the beat. Polymeter needs the second loop to be heard.
3. Try **7 against 8**. A seven-step riff over a bar of eight slips back by one step every bar. It takes eight bars (56 steps) to come round.
4. Try **13 against 16**. The readout says 208 steps (thirteen bars) before it lines up again: longer than most listeners can hold. Somewhere along the way, the riff stops sounding "wrong" and starts sounding like its own layer.
5. Edit the figure by clicking its cells, and change **Figure length**. Find a length that never lines up within a bar of 16 until 16 figures have passed (any odd length will do). Why do odd lengths take so long?
</Callout>

<Callout kind="check">
A riff five steps long repeats over a drum pattern eight steps long, both on the same sixteenth-note pulse. After how many steps do they line up again, and how many times will the riff have repeated?

<Answer>
The least common multiple of 5 and 8 is 40, so after 40 steps: eight repeats of the riff and five bars of eight. If the bar is a bar of 4/4 in eighth notes, that's five bars; in sixteenths (16 steps per bar), it's 2½ bars, so the true realignment with the *bar line* comes at 80 steps, the least common multiple of 5 and 16.
</Answer>
</Callout>

### Polymeter in rock and metal

Rock bands discovered polymeter as a way to build hypnotic, unsettling textures from simple loops. In the early-1980s King Crimson, the guitarists Robert Fripp and Adrian Belew played interlocking parts of different lengths, influenced partly by Balinese gamelan (chapter 1.4's kotekan) and minimalism, so that the two guitars continually realigned. The Swedish metal band Meshuggah took the idea to an extreme: the drummer's cymbals keep a plain 4/4, while the guitars and kick drum play long riffs built from uneven groups that only realign with the bar after many bars.

### A note on the words

The terms in this chapter are used inconsistently, even by music theorists. Some writers use "polymeter" for two parts with *different time signatures but the same bar length* (3/4 against 6/8, which is really 3 : 2), and "polyrhythm" for everything. Others use "cross-rhythm" and "polyrhythm" interchangeably. Justin London (2012) argues that true polymeter, two meters genuinely heard at once, may be rare or impossible for listeners, for the same reason two beats are hard to hold (Poudrier and Repp's single-framework finding): what we hear is one meter with a pattern of a different length against it.

The distinctions in this chapter are useful even if the vocabulary isn't standard:

## Phasing

### One pattern, two positions

The last kind of disagreement uses one pattern twice. Steve Reich discovered it by accident in 1965, playing two tape loops of the same recorded phrase on two machines. The machines ran at very slightly different speeds, so one loop gradually pulled ahead of the other. As it did, the two copies passed through every possible offset, and each offset produced a new composite pattern from the same material.

Chapter 1.1 described **phase** as a position within a repeating cycle, and showed two identical waves adding up differently depending on their offset. **Phasing** is the same idea on the scale of a rhythm or melody: same repeating shape, different offset, different result.

Reich then asked live musicians to do what the tape machines had done. In *Piano Phase* (1967), two pianists play the same quick twelve-note pattern in unison. One of them speeds up very slightly until they're exactly one note ahead, then holds that position for a while; then drifts again to two notes ahead, and so on, until after twelve steps they are back in unison. In *Clapping Music* (1972), Reich removed the drifting: two performers clap the same twelve-pulse pattern, and after a set number of repetitions, one of them jumps ahead by one pulse.

<Callout kind="check">
In the lab's sudden-steps mode, after the moving part has shifted twelve times, it's back in unison. Why twelve, and why does the composite pattern at offset 6 sound different from the one at offset 1?

<Answer>
The pattern is twelve pulses long, so shifting by twelve pulses moves it a whole cycle, which is the same as no shift at all, like a wave delayed by one full period (chapter 1.1). Each offset lines up different strokes of the pattern with each other. At offset 1, each stroke meets its neighbor; at offset 6, each stroke meets the stroke half a cycle away. Different strokes coincide or interleave, so the combined rhythm has a different number of sounding pulses and a different shape.
</Answer>
</Callout>

## Why build music on disagreement?

Why do so many traditions deliberately layer rhythms that don't line up? A few reasons, some well supported, some more speculative:

- **It keeps a simple cycle interesting.** A twelve-pulse cycle, a four-step bar, a twelve-note pattern: each is simple on its own. Layered against something it disagrees with, it generates new patterns on every repeat, so the music can repeat for a long time without going stale (chapter 1.6 on habituation).
- **It gives participants roles.** In an ensemble where each part has its own relationship to the timeline, each player and dancer can hold a different layer, and the whole emerges from their coordination. This is about social structure as much as sound.
- **It rewards attention.** A listener can move their attention between layers and hear a different piece each time, which may be why polyrhythmic textures sustain repeated listening.
- **It sits on the edge of what the listener can hold.** Too simple and it's predictable; too complex and it falls apart into noise. Chapter 2.5 takes up research suggesting that a moderate amount of rhythmic conflict is what makes people most want to move.

<Callout kind="verify" title="why layering">
These reasons are interpretive, not established findings. The link to habituation is my inference; the "roles" argument is common in ethnomusicology (e.g. Chernoff 1979, *African Rhythm and African Sensibility*; Locke); the "moderate complexity" claim is developed in chapter 2.5 (Witek et al. 2014).
</Callout>

- **Examples.** Swap in your own: Fela Kuti's Afrobeat, Radiohead ("15 Step", "Pyramid Song"), Tool ("Schism"), Brahms's hemiolas and cross-rhythms, Elvin Jones's drumming with Coltrane, Indian tala's tihai (a figure repeated three times to land on the downbeat, which is a kind of polymeter).

**Misconceptions to watch for:**

- "Polyrhythm means complicated rhythm." It means two pulses at once; 3 : 2 is simple and everywhere.
- "Listeners hear both pulses equally." Usually one is the beat and the other is heard against it.
- "African music is polyrhythmic and European music isn't." Both have cross-rhythm; the difference is in how central it is, and "African music" isn't one tradition.
- "Polymeter means two time signatures." Usage varies; the useful distinction is shared pulse with different cycle lengths.
- "Phasing is out-of-time playing." It's exact: every offset is a precise relationship, passed through in order.
</Callout>
