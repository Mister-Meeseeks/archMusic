## The question

Play the white keys of a piano, any order, any rhythm, for a minute. Which note is home?

There's no answer in the notes. The seven white keys are the C major scale, but they are also the A minor scale, and five other scales besides, depending on which one you treat as home. A melody on the white keys that keeps returning to C, rests on C, and ends on C sounds like C major. The same keys, circling around A and ending on A, sound like A minor: same notes, darker mood. Circle around D instead and you get something that is neither, a sound you know from "Scarborough Fair," Santana, and Miles Davis.

So the <Term id="tonic">tonic</Term> is not a property of a set of notes. It's something your brain *infers* from how the notes are used, and once inferred, it reorganizes everything: each note becomes stable or unstable, near home or far from it. This chapter is about that inference and the structure it creates. Four ideas:

1. **Home is inferred** from repetition, duration, position, the bass, and a few telltale intervals.
2. **The notes of a key form a hierarchy**, which listeners can rate with surprising consistency, and which largely mirrors how often each note occurs.
3. **Much of the hierarchy is learned**, from exposure, which is why it differs between musical cultures and why a computer can approximate it by counting.
4. **Moving home within the same notes gives the modes**, each with its own color, and drones and pedal points are the most direct ways to say where home is.

## What makes a note sound like home

### The cues

Composers and improvisers establish a tonic with a handful of devices, mostly without thinking about it. Researchers have tested each one:

- **Repetition and duration.** The note heard most often, and held longest, is a strong candidate. Melodies spend a lot of time on their tonic.
- **Position.** First and last notes matter, last most of all: chapter 3.3 showed that tunes overwhelmingly end on the tonic. Notes on strong beats count more than notes between beats.
- **The bass.** The lowest note carries more weight than the others. A held low note is the bluntest way to name home, which is why drones work (below).
- **The tonic chord.** The major or minor chord built on the tonic, sounded at the start and end, confirms it.
- **Telltale intervals.** Some intervals occur in only one or two places in a scale. In the major scale, the two half steps (E–F and B–C) and the one tritone (F–B) pin down where you are. Hearing B go up a half step to C points at C as home in a way that, say, D going to E does not. This is the *rare-interval* idea of Brown and Butler.

None of these is decisive alone, and they can conflict. A well-made tune lines them up: "Twinkle, Twinkle" starts on C, ends on C, sits on C and G, and outlines the C major chord in its first leap. A tune that keeps them in conflict sounds ambiguous, which can be a feature.

### Measuring the hierarchy: probe tones

In 1979 and 1982, Carol Krumhansl and her colleagues found a way to ask listeners about home without using any music theory vocabulary. Play a short passage that sets up a key, such as a chord progression or a scale. Then play one single tone, the *probe*. Ask: on a scale of 1 to 7, how well does this tone fit? Repeat for all twelve notes, in many keys, with many listeners.

Try it yourself before reading on. Rate every note at least once in C major; the rating buttons appear after each tone.

Read it as levels:

1. **The tonic** (C) fits best, by a wide margin.
2. **The fifth** (G) comes next, then **the third** (E in major, E♭ in minor). Together with the tonic these are the notes of the tonic chord.
3. **The other scale notes** come next.
4. **The five notes outside the scale** fit worst.

This is the hierarchy every chapter on harmony builds on: once a key is established, every note has a *place*, closer to home or farther from it. Chapter 3.3's observation that melodies end on the tonic, and that a phrase stopping on degree 2 sounds like a question, are both consequences of it.

<Answer>
The probe-tone task asks how well a note fits as an *ending*, more or less: how stable it is. B is unstable, which is exactly why it pulls. An unstable note near a very stable one sounds like it should move there. Stability and pull are two sides of the same hierarchy: the lower a note's rating, the more it sounds like it is on its way somewhere, and when a stable note is a half step away, the direction is obvious. Chapter 3.6 makes this pull the engine of Western harmony.
</Answer>

### Where the hierarchy comes from

Two explanations compete, and both are partly right.

**Statistics.** The hierarchy matches how often each note actually occurs in Western tonal music. Krumhansl compared her profiles with note counts from pieces by Schubert, Mozart, and others, and the correlation was very high: the tonic is the most common note, then the fifth and third, and so on. On this view, listeners learn the hierarchy the way they learn the vocabulary of a language, by absorbing what usually happens. That is the <Term id="statistical-learning">statistical learning</Term> idea, applied to pitch.

**Acoustics.** The tonic, fifth, and third are also the notes most closely related through the harmonic series (chapter 1.2): the fifth and the third are low harmonics of the tonic. So the hierarchy might partly follow from consonance, which is shared by all listeners, rather than from exposure. Models of the auditory system that know nothing about music can reproduce some of the probe-tone results from the sound alone. Leman (2000) argued that much of a probe-tone rating can be explained by short-term memory: how much the probe resembles what was *just played*. That's a more deflationary story: listeners may be matching the probe to the recent sound rather than consulting a learned, abstract key.

The evidence that learning matters a lot:

- **Other cultures, other hierarchies.** Castellano, Bharucha, and Krumhansl (1984) played North Indian rāgs to Indian and Western listeners. Both groups produced hierarchies that followed which notes the passages emphasized, but only the Indian listeners' ratings also reflected the rāg's traditional structure beyond the passage itself. Studies with Balinese music found similar patterns (Kessler, Hansen & Shepard, 1984).
- **New scales, learned in minutes.** Loui, Wessel, and Hudson Kam (2010) built melodies from a scale unrelated to any existing musical system. After half an hour of listening, people's ratings of new melodies followed the frequencies of the notes they had heard.
- **Children.** Young children's sense of key develops gradually through the early school years, in step with their exposure to music.

The fair summary: some notes are acoustically privileged, which may be why the fifth and the octave turn up in so many cultures' scales, and experience builds a detailed, culture-specific hierarchy on top.

### Teaching a computer to find the key

If the hierarchy mostly mirrors how often notes occur, a computer should be able to find the key just by counting. Krumhansl and Schmuckler's **key-finding** method does exactly that: add up how long each of the twelve pitch classes sounds in a passage, then compare the result with the probe-tone profile of each of the 24 major and minor keys. The key whose profile matches best wins.

<ModeLab initialMode="keyfind" modes={['keyfind']} />

<Callout kind="try">
1. Start with **Twinkle, Twinkle** and **Happy Birthday**. The method gets both right, confidently.
2. Try **Mary Had a Little Lamb** and **Jingle Bells**. The method says E minor. Look at the bars: both tunes spend most of their time on E, so by the numbers, E looks like home. You hear C major because both tunes *end* on C, and because of the order of the notes, which the method ignores.
3. Try **Beethoven 5**. Only four different pitches, so the evidence is thin and the method's guesses are all weak.
4. Try **Drunken Sailor**, a sea shanty in D Dorian (next section). The method only knows major and minor, so it has no right answer available.
</Callout>

Counting gets surprisingly far, and its failures are instructive. Listeners use *order* and *position* as well as totals: the last note, the notes on strong beats, the rare intervals. Later key-finding models (Temperley's, for instance) add some of this and do better. And all of these models are built for one tradition's two kinds of key. Which brings us to the modes.

## Same notes, different home: the modes

### The white-key experiment

Go back to the opening question. Take the seven white keys and choose each one in turn as home. You get seven different scales, called the **modes**. Each has the same notes, but the half steps fall in different places relative to home, and so each sounds different.

<ModeLab initialMode="modes" modes={['modes']} mode="ionian" />

<Callout kind="try">
1. With **Same notes, new home** selected, play **A tune** in **Ionian** (C to C). It's an ordinary major-key tune.
2. While it plays, click **Aeolian** (A to A). The drone drops to A, the same phrase now uses only the white keys from A, and it turns into a minor-key tune.
3. Click through the rest. Listen for the orange key: the note that gives each mode its particular color.
4. Turn the drone off and switch modes again. Without the drone, the difference is much weaker, because nothing is telling your ear where home is except the tune itself.
</Callout>

The names are borrowed from ancient Greek, by way of medieval church music, and don't mean what the Greeks meant by them. Two of the modes you already know: **Ionian** is the major scale, and **Aeolian** is the natural minor scale. The others are best understood as major or minor with one or two notes changed:

| Mode | White keys | Compared with major | Color | Where you hear it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Lydian** | F to F | ♯4 | Bright, floating, dreamy | The Simpsons theme; much film music |
| **Ionian** (major) | C to C | none | Bright, settled | Most of the songs you know |
| **Mixolydian** | G to G | ♭7 | Major but loose, bluesy | "Norwegian Wood"; a great deal of rock and folk |
| **Dorian** | D to D | ♭3, ♭7 | Minor but not sad; cool | "Scarborough Fair"; "Oye Como Va"; "So What" |
| **Aeolian** (natural minor) | A to A | ♭3, ♭6, ♭7 | Minor, darker | "All Along the Watchtower" |

</DataTable>

Read the table, or the ladder figure, from top to bottom: each mode lowers exactly one more note. That ordering is a useful way to hear them. Lydian is major with an extra-bright raised 4th; Locrian is so dark that its home note can barely hold, because the fifth above it is a tritone, not a perfect fifth, so the chord built on home (B–D–F) is neither major nor minor but an unstable, clashing one.

<Callout kind="check">
Using the hierarchy from the probe-tone section: why might Locrian be so rarely used as a real home, even in traditions that use the other six modes freely?

<Answer>
In the hierarchy, the most stable notes after the tonic are the fifth and the third, and together they form the tonic chord. Locrian has no perfect fifth above its tonic (B to F is a tritone), so the chord built on its tonic (B–D–F) is neither major nor minor but a rough, unstable one. The second most important support for home is missing. Listeners tend to hear a passage on the white keys centered on B as being "really" in C or E, with B as a temporary resting point. Locrian passages exist, especially in metal and jazz, but usually over a held bass note that insists on home.
</Answer>
</Callout>

### Same home, different notes: relative and parallel

There are two ways to compare scales, and the vocabulary for them is worth getting straight.

- **Relative** major and minor share *notes* but not *home*. C major and A minor both use the white keys. Every major key has a **relative minor** three semitones below its tonic, and vice versa.
- **Parallel** major and minor share *home* but not *notes*. C major and C minor both have C as tonic, but C minor lowers the 3rd, 6th, and 7th.

The white-key experiment above compared relative modes. For hearing a mode's *character*, the parallel comparison is usually clearer: keep home fixed and change only the notes, so you hear exactly what the altered note does.

<ModeLab initialMode="modes" modes={['modes']} compare="parallel" mode="dorian" />

<Callout kind="try">
1. Play **The scale** with **Dorian** selected (home on C, so C D E♭ F G A B♭). Then click **Aeolian**. The only change is A becoming A♭. Hear how the A♭ darkens the minor sound and the A lifts it.
2. Go from **Ionian** to **Mixolydian**: only B becomes B♭. The scale loses its upward lean into home.
3. From **Ionian** to **Lydian**: only F becomes F♯. It sounds slightly suspended, as if not quite on the ground.
</Callout>

Songs move between parallel modes freely. Rock in particular borrows the lowered 7th and 3rd from the parallel minor while keeping a major tonic chord, which is why so much rock sounds neither purely major nor purely minor. "Norwegian Wood" sets a Mixolydian opening section against a middle section that shifts toward the parallel minor.

### A short history of the modes

Medieval church music classified chants into eight modes, by their final note and range. In 1547 the theorist Heinrich Glarean added Ionian and Aeolian, which was a description of what musicians were already doing. Over the next century and a half, European music came to rely on just those two, major and minor, mainly because they suit harmony built on chords and the strong pull toward home that chapter 3.6 describes.

The other modes never disappeared. They survived in folk music (much English, Irish, and Scottish folk song is Dorian or Mixolydian), came back into concert music with composers like Debussy and Vaughan Williams, became the basis of modal jazz, and are everywhere in rock, film, and video-game music. Today a film composer reaches for Lydian to make something sound wondrous, and a metal guitarist reaches for Phrygian to make it sound menacing, as deliberately as a painter picks a color.

## Saying where home is: drones and pedal points

### The drone

The most direct way to establish home is to sound it continuously. A **drone** is a note, usually with its fifth, held under an entire piece or section. Every other note is then heard *against* it, as a specific distance from home, and the mode becomes unmistakable. (You heard this in the Modes lab: turning the drone off weakened the difference between modes.)

Drones are among the oldest and most widespread musical devices: bagpipes have drone pipes; the Indian *tanpura* surrounds every performance of classical Indian music with a shimmering drone on the home note (*sa*) and usually the fifth (*pa*); medieval organum, Appalachian dulcimers, and the hurdy-gurdy all have them.

<ListeningCue track="shankar-jogeshwari-alap" start="0:00" end="2:00">
The opening of a rāg performance, the *ālāp*, introduces the rāg's notes one at a time over the tanpura drone, with no beat. Listen to how each new note sounds like a specific color against the unchanging home note, and how certain notes are returned to and dwelt on. A rāg is more than a scale: it specifies which notes are important, which phrases are characteristic, and how notes are approached. In probe-tone terms, it specifies a hierarchy.
</ListeningCue>

<Callout kind="verify" title="drones and rāg">

</Callout>

### The pedal point

A **pedal point** is the same idea used temporarily. A note is held or repeated in the bass while the harmony above it moves, including through chords that don't contain it. The name comes from the organ, where a player can hold a low note with a foot. A pedal on the tonic, especially at the start or end of a piece, nails down home while the music above wanders. Pop and rock use the same device whenever the bass stays on one note under changing chords, and electronic dance music often holds a single bass note for many bars.

Chapter 5.4 uses pedal points the other way round: held on a note *other* than the tonic, they build expectation for home.

## The pentatonic scale

Not every scale has seven notes. The most widespread alternative has five: the **pentatonic scale**. Play only the black keys of a piano and you are playing one. On C, it's C D E G A: the major scale with its two half steps removed (F and B).

With no half steps, the pentatonic scale has no notes that lean hard into a neighbor. That has two consequences:

- **It's hard to play a wrong note.** Any combination sounds reasonable, which is why improvising on the black keys sounds fine and why pentatonic tunes are so singable. "Amazing Grace" and "Auld Lang Syne" are pentatonic.
- **Home is set by the tune, not the scale.** The same five notes can center on C (major pentatonic) or on A (A C D E G, the **minor pentatonic**). The minor pentatonic is the backbone of blues and rock guitar soloing.

Pentatonic scales appear in folk and traditional music across much of the world, from Scotland to West Africa to East Asia, though often tuned differently from the piano's. Chapter 3.2 asks why five- and seven-note scales turn up so widely.

## How strong is home?

The hierarchy can be strong or weak. A Bach chorale or a pop ballad establishes its key within a few seconds and never lets you forget it. A modal jazz solo, a raga, or a drone-based piece holds home steady while making the other notes vivid. Some music deliberately keeps home uncertain: an ambiguous melody, a pentatonic vamp that could be on either of two notes. Some twentieth-century composers abandoned the tonic altogether, writing music in which no note is home; chapter 3.9 asks what listeners hear in it.

Even within one piece, home can move. That's the subject of chapter 3.6.

- "Minor means sad." Dorian is minor and often sounds cool or bright; mood depends on tempo, register, timbre, and context too.
- "The tonal hierarchy is universal." The tonic-and-fifth part may be widely shared; the detail is learned.
- "Modes are exotic." Two of them are major and minor, and the others are in a lot of the music you already know.
- "A low probe-tone rating means a bad note." It means an unstable note, which is what makes melody move.
</Callout>
