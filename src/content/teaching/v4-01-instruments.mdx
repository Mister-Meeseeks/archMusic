## The question

A violinist and a clarinetist play the same note, at the same loudness. You can tell them apart instantly, with your eyes closed, from the far end of a hall. Chapter 1.2 gave the first answer: their notes have different recipes of <Term id="harmonics">harmonics</Term>. But why? Where does a recipe come from?

The answer is that each instrument is a small physical machine, and the machine decides the recipe. Some of its parts are as simple as a stretched wire; some are as subtle as the curve of a brass bell. Instrument makers spent centuries learning, mostly by trial and error, how to shape those parts so that physics produces something musical. Seen this way, a Stradivarius and a marimba are feats of engineering as much as works of craft.

This chapter takes the machines apart. The aim isn't to cover every instrument, but to give you a small set of principles that explain most of them.

## Exciter, resonator, radiator

Almost every acoustic instrument has three parts:

- An **exciter** puts energy in: a bow, a finger plucking, a felt hammer, a stick, a reed, a player's buzzing lips, a jet of breath.
- A **resonator** decides which frequencies vibrate: a string, a column of air inside a tube, a stretched skin, a metal bar. Its modes, the <Term id="standing-wave">standing-wave</Term> patterns it naturally supports, set the possible frequencies.
- A **radiator** hands the vibration on to the air: a wooden soundboard, a guitar's body, a trumpet's bell, a drum's shell.

The three parts work very differently in two families of instruments:

- In **struck and plucked** instruments (piano, guitar, harp, drums, bells), the exciter acts once and gets out of the way. The resonator then rings on its own, in its own modes, and the sound dies away.
- In **bowed and blown** instruments (violin family, woodwinds, brass, the voice), the exciter keeps pumping energy in for as long as the note lasts. And the resonator *talks back*: its vibration controls the exciter. That feedback loop turns out to matter enormously, as the next sections show.

## Strings

### Why strings are harmonic, and how they're set going

Chapter 1.2 showed the essentials. A string clamped at both ends can only vibrate in patterns that fit a whole number of half-waves between the ends, so its modes sit at 1×, 2×, 3×... the lowest frequency: a harmonic series. A pluck sets all the modes going at once, in proportions that depend on where you pluck. Pluck near the end and the high harmonics are strong (a bright, twangy sound); pluck in the middle and the sound is round and soft, with every even harmonic missing, because they all have a still point (a node) exactly in the middle.

The three ways of exciting a string give three characteristic sounds:

- **Plucking** (guitar, harp, harpsichord) pulls the string into a triangle and lets go. The harmonics fade at roughly 1/*n*², and the higher ones die away fastest, so a plucked note starts bright and mellows as it decays.
- **Striking** (piano) throws a felt hammer at the string, which bounces off within a few thousandths of a second. A soft hammer, or a gentle blow, stays in contact longer and excites mainly the low modes; a hard blow from the same hammer is brighter. That's why a loud piano note isn't just a louder version of a soft one: its *spectrum* changes. Piano makers also place the hammer about one-seventh to one-ninth of the way along the string, roughly where the jarring 7th and 9th harmonics have their nodes.
- **Bowing** is the strange one.

The "one-seventh to one-ninth of the string, to weaken the 7th and 9th harmonics" claim is traditional and often repeated, but how much it actually matters is debated (the hammer's width and contact time also shape the spectrum). Check against Fletcher & Rossing (1998), chapter 12, or Askenfelt's piano research before keeping it.
</Callout>

### The bow: stick and slip

Pull a bow across a string. Rosin makes the hair sticky, so the hair grips the string and drags it sideways. The string's tension pulls back harder and harder until, suddenly, the hair loses its grip and the string snaps back the other way, sliding under the hair. Then the hair grips again, and the cycle repeats: **stick, slip, stick, slip**, hundreds of times a second.

What's remarkable is the shape the string takes. Hermann von Helmholtz, watching a bowed string through a vibrating microscope in the 1860s, found that it doesn't wobble in a smooth curve. It forms two straight lines meeting at a sharp corner, and the corner races around the string, once per period, along a curved path. When the corner passes the bow, it knocks the string loose (slip); when it comes back, the string is recaptured (stick). This is now called **Helmholtz motion**. The force it puts on the bridge is a **sawtooth wave**, the waveform with every harmonic at strength 1/*n*: the bright, buzzy raw material a violin starts from.

### Feedback makes perfect harmonics

Here is the deep point about bowed and blown instruments. In a plucked string, each mode vibrates on its own, at its own natural frequency. If those frequencies were slightly out of a perfect harmonic series, the sound would simply contain slightly mistuned partials.

A bowed string can't do that. The moment of slip is triggered by the corner returning to the bow, once per period. The whole motion is one pattern that repeats exactly, and a waveform that repeats exactly has, by definition, partials at *exact* whole-number multiples of its repetition rate. The bow forces the string's modes into lockstep. The same thing happens in wind instruments, where the pressure in the air column decides when the reed opens. This is called **mode locking**, and it's why sustained instruments (strings bowed, winds, brass, voices) produce perfectly harmonic tones, while struck and plucked ones are harmonic only as far as their resonators happen to be.

You'll hear the difference in the lab below. Make the string stiff, which pulls its modes slightly out of line, then compare plucking it with bowing it.

### The body: why a bare string is nearly silent

Stretch a string between two nails on a heavy table and pluck it. You'll barely hear it. A string is so thin that it slices through the air almost without pushing any of it: the air simply flows around it. A string by itself is a terrible radiator.

So stringed instruments pass the vibration on. The string presses on a **bridge**, which rocks a large, thin, light plate: a piano's **soundboard**, the top of a violin or guitar. The plate moves a lot of air. But a plate is a resonator too, with modes of its own, and so is the air inside a hollow body. The body therefore acts as a **filter**: it boosts the string's harmonics near its own resonances and weakens others, just as the vocal tract shapes the buzz of the vocal folds into <Term id="formant">formants</Term> (chapter 1.2).

These body resonances are a large part of what makes a violin a violin and a guitar a guitar, and a good violin different from a mediocre one. A violin has a few strong low resonances (the air in the body sloshing in and out of the f-holes, and the main modes of the wooden plates) and a group of resonances around 2–3 kHz, sometimes called the "bridge hill", which gives brilliance. Occasionally a body resonance is so strong that it fights the string, and one note comes out unsteady and growling: cellists call it the **wolf note**.

### Stiff strings and stretched octaves

An ideal string bends freely. A real piano string is thick steel wire, and steel resists bending. For the low modes, which bend the string gently, this hardly matters. But the high modes divide the string into short segments with sharp bends, and the wire's stiffness pushes back, making them vibrate a little *faster* than the ideal. Mode 2 is a hair sharp of 2×, mode 10 noticeably sharp of 10×, and the error grows with the square of the mode number. Harvey Fletcher wrote it down in 1964: the *n*th mode sits at *n* × *f* × √(1 + *Bn*²), where *B* measures the string's stiffness.

That's the **inharmonicity** chapter 1.2 mentioned. Its consequences run through the whole design of the piano:

- **Stretched tuning.** If a tuner set each octave to an exact 2:1, the second mode of the lower note (slightly sharp of 2×) would clash with the fundamental of the upper note, producing slow beating. So tuners widen each octave slightly to match the partials. Across the keyboard the stretch accumulates: the top notes end up something like 20–30 <Term id="cents">cents</Term> sharp of equal temperament, and the bottom notes a similar amount flat. Plotted against key number, that's the famous **Railsback curve** (1938).
- **Wound bass strings.** To make a low note, a string needs to be long, heavy, or slack. A piano can't be ten meters long, so its bass strings are wrapped in copper wire, which adds mass without adding much stiffness.
- **Why big pianos sound better in the bass.** A concert grand's bass strings are longer than an upright's, so they can be thinner and less stiff for the same pitch. Their partials are closer to harmonic, and the low notes sound clearer and less "tubby".

### Sympathetic resonance

Press the sustain pedal on a piano, lifting all the dampers, and sing a loud note into it. When you stop, the piano sings your note back: every string whose modes match frequencies in your voice has picked up energy and started to ring. This is **sympathetic resonance**, resonance driven by a sound arriving from outside.

Pianists use it constantly: the pedal doesn't just sustain notes, it adds a halo of sympathetically ringing strings. Some instruments are built around it. The Indian sitar has a set of thin *taraf* strings under the main ones that are never played directly; they're tuned to the notes of the raga and shimmer whenever the melody touches them. The Baroque viola d'amore and the Norwegian Hardanger fiddle have sympathetic strings too.

## Air columns

### Open and closed pipes

In a wind instrument the resonator is the column of air inside a tube. Air in a tube supports standing waves just as a string does, but with a twist: what happens at the ends depends on whether they're open or closed.

- At an **open end**, the air is free to rush in and out, so the air moves most there (the pressure stays close to the room's).
- At a **closed end**, the air can't move at all, so that's a still point for the air's motion.

A tube **open at both ends**, like a flute (open at the far end and, effectively, at the mouth hole), has free ends at both sides, and its standing waves fit the same way a string's do, just mirrored: a whole number of half-waves. Its modes form the complete harmonic series, 1, 2, 3, 4...

A tube **closed at one end** is different. The pattern has to be still at the closed end and free at the open end. The lowest pattern that fits is a quarter of a wave, so the fundamental is an **octave lower** than an open tube of the same length. And the next patterns that fit are three quarters, five quarters, seven quarters...: only the **odd harmonics**, 1, 3, 5, 7.

The clarinet is the classic closed pipe. Its tube is a cylinder, and the reed end acts as closed (more on why in a moment). That explains two famous clarinet facts at once: its low notes are surprisingly deep for its length (a clarinet and a flute are roughly the same length, but the clarinet reaches about an octave lower), and its low register has a hollow, woody tone with weak even harmonics. That's the "Hollow" recipe from chapter 1.2's harmonic builder.

### Edges, reeds, and lips

Each kind of wind instrument has its own exciter, and each one works as a valve controlled by the air column:

- **Air jets (flutes, recorders, organ flue pipes).** The player blows a thin jet of air across a sharp edge. The jet flips back and forth, in and out of the tube, and the air column's pressure tells it when to flip. Because the mouth hole is open to the room, the flute behaves as a pipe open at both ends.
- **Cane reeds.** A **single reed** (clarinet, saxophone) is a thin blade of cane that beats against the mouthpiece; a **double reed** (oboe, bassoon) is two blades beating against each other. Either way, the reed is a valve that opens and closes on the player's breath, chopping it into puffs. When the pressure in the mouthpiece swings high, it pushes the reed open, and so on: the air column drives the reed. Because the reed end is almost sealed most of the time, it behaves acoustically as a closed end.
- **Lip reeds (brass).** A brass player's lips buzz in the mouthpiece, opening and closing like a reed. The air column again controls when they open.

In every case the resonator controls the exciter, and that's mode locking again: as long as the player sustains a note, its partials are exactly harmonic.

### Jumping between modes: overblowing

Blow harder or tighter into a wind instrument, and at some point it jumps up to a higher mode of its air column. That's **overblowing**, and it's how winds cover their range:

- A **flute** overblows to its second mode, the **octave**. Its fingerings repeat, one octave up.
- A **clarinet** has no second mode (no even harmonics), so it jumps to its third, an **octave and a fifth** (a twelfth) up. A small hole near the top, the **register key**, helps it make the jump. Its fingerings repeat a twelfth higher, which is why clarinet fingering is notoriously different from other woodwinds.
- **Brass instruments** take overblowing to the extreme. A bugle has no valves at all: by changing lip tension, a bugler picks out modes 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 of a single tube, which is why bugle calls ("Taps", "Reveille") use only the notes of a major chord. Valves (on trumpets and horns) and slides (on trombones) add extra lengths of tubing, giving several harmonic series to choose from, and the gaps between their modes are filled in.

### Cones and bells

Two more pieces explain the rest of the wind family.

**Cones.** The oboe, bassoon, and saxophone have reeds, like the clarinet, but their tubes are **conical**, widening steadily from the reed to the end. Remarkably, a cone closed at its narrow end has the *same* modes as an open cylinder: the full harmonic series. So a saxophone, despite its single reed and clarinet-like mouthpiece, overblows to the octave and has all its harmonics, and it sounds more like an oboe's cousin than a clarinet's. The **bore**, the shape of the inside of the tube, matters more than the exciter.

**Brass bells and mouthpieces.** A trumpet is mostly a cylinder, closed at the lips, which should give odd harmonics only. But the flaring bell at one end and the cup-shaped mouthpiece at the other shift the modes, and makers have refined those shapes over two centuries until the modes line up almost exactly with a full harmonic series (all but the lowest, which is out of place and rarely used). The bell also works as a radiator, and a filter: it lets high frequencies out efficiently and reflects low ones back into the tube. Played loudly, brass instruments generate many strong upper harmonics, which is why a loud trumpet isn't just louder than a soft one but blazingly brighter.

## Membranes, bars, and bells

### The drumhead

A drumhead is a stretched membrane, fixed around its rim: the two-dimensional version of a string. And going from one dimension to two breaks the neat whole-number pattern completely.

A drumhead's modes divide its surface into regions moving in opposite directions, separated by still **nodal lines**: straight **diameters** across the head and **circles** around its center. The simplest mode has the whole head bulging up and down together. The next has one nodal diameter, with one half going up while the other goes down. And so on. Their frequencies come from the mathematics of circles (the zeros of Bessel functions), and they're nowhere near whole-number multiples: for an ideal head, the first few modes are at 1, 1.59, 2.14, 2.30, 2.65, 2.92 times the lowest. That's why most drums have no clear pitch: their modes don't fit any harmonic series, so the brain can't find one fundamental to hear (chapter 1.4's grouping by harmonicity fails).

Where you strike matters, because a stick can only excite modes that move at the point it hits:

- **Dead center**, every mode with a nodal diameter has a node there, so only the circular modes sound. These modes also radiate sound very efficiently, which means they lose their energy fast. The result is a short, dull thud.
- **Near the edge**, many more modes sound, and the note rings.

That's why drummers and timpanists rarely hit the exact center. A snare drum adds one more trick: a set of wires (the **snares**) stretched across the bottom head buzz against it on every hit, adding a burst of noise. The snare's crack in a <Term id="drum-kit">drum kit</Term> is mostly that buzz.

### Timpani: tuning a drum

The orchestral timpano is a membrane stretched over a large copper bowl, and it produces a clear, tunable pitch. How, when membranes are inharmonic? Lord Rayleigh puzzled over it in the nineteenth century, and the answer is the air. The head has to push the air in the kettle and the air around it, and that extra load lowers the modes' frequencies, the low ones most. In a well-made timpano, the modes with one, two, three, four, and five nodal diameters end up close to the ratios **2 : 3 : 4 : 5 : 6**, like harmonics of a note an octave below the main one. The bulging-up-and-down mode, which would wreck the harmony, radiates so efficiently that it dies almost instantly, and the timpanist strikes about a quarter of the way in from the rim to excite it as little as possible.

Curiously, listeners usually hear the timpano's pitch as the frequency of its strongest mode (the one with one nodal diameter, called the *principal tone*), not as the missing fundamental an octave below it, even though the modes above line up like a harmonic series built on that lower note.

### The tabla: a drum that sings

Indian drummers solved the same problem differently, centuries earlier. The right-hand drum of the tabla (the *dayan*) has a thick black patch in the middle of its head, the *syahi*, built up from layers of a paste of iron filings, soot, and starch, and carefully shaped by the maker. That extra mass in the center changes the shapes of the modes and moves their frequencies. In 1934 the physicist C. V. Raman (by then a Nobel laureate for his work on light) showed that the loaded head brings the first nine modes into five groups at very nearly **1 : 2 : 3 : 4 : 5**: a harmonic series, from a drum. The tabla is tuned to the tonal center of the music, and it rings with a clear pitch.

The left-hand drum (the *bayan*) is larger, with an off-center patch, and the player presses the heel of the hand into its head while it sounds, raising the tension and making the pitch swoop upward.

### Bars and how makers tune them

A metal or wooden bar, free at both ends (held up only by soft supports), is the resonator of the glockenspiel, xylophone, marimba, vibraphone, and the metallophones of the Indonesian gamelan. A bar resists bending even more strongly than a piano string does, and its modes climb very fast: **1 : 2.76 : 5.40 : 8.93**. Strike a plain bar and you hear a clear pitch from mode 1, plus high, clashing overtones that die away quickly. That bright "ping" is the sound of a glockenspiel.

Marimba and xylophone makers tame the bar by carving an arch out of its underside. Removing wood from the middle makes the bar more flexible exactly where mode 1 bends it most, lowering mode 1 relative to the others. By shaping the arch carefully, a maker can put mode 2 exactly **two octaves** above mode 1 on a marimba (4:1), or an **octave and a fifth** above on a xylophone (3:1); the best makers tune mode 3 as well. Bars are hung at the two nodes of mode 1, where they barely move, so the supports don't damp the note. And under each marimba bar hangs a tube, a closed pipe tuned to the bar's fundamental, which resonates and makes the note louder and warmer.

### Bells

A church bell is the most elaborate resonator in this chapter: a thick, curved shell of bronze that vibrates in dozens of modes, with nodal lines running down its sides (meridians) and around it (circles). Bell founders gave the important ones names. In a well-tuned bell, measured from the **prime**:

- the **hum** sits an octave below,
- the **tierce** a minor third above,
- the **quint** a fifth above,
- the **nominal** an octave above,
- and more above that.

That minor third, built into every tuned bell, is what gives bells their faintly sad, plaintive sound, even when a carillon plays a cheerful major-key melody.

The puzzle is the pitch. When you hear a bell struck, you usually hear a **strike note**, close to the prime, but it often isn't produced by the prime mode at all. It's a pitch the brain constructs from the upper partials, the way it constructs the missing fundamental (chapter 1.3) from harmonics. Tuning bells so that the modes line up this well is a lost-and-found art: the Hemony brothers mastered it in the seventeenth-century Netherlands, and it was largely forgotten until bellfounders rediscovered it around 1900, cutting metal from the inside of the bell on a lathe while measuring the modes against tuning forks.

<Callout kind="check">
A guitarist plays a note, then plays the same note as a "harmonic" by touching the string lightly at its midpoint while plucking. The second note is an octave higher and sounds purer. Using what you know about modes and nodes, explain both facts.

<Answer>
Touching the string at its midpoint forces a node there. Only modes that already have a node at the midpoint can survive: the even modes (2, 4, 6...). The lowest surviving mode is mode 2, an octave above the open string, so that's the pitch you hear. And with the odd modes gone, there are fewer harmonics in the sound (the remaining ones form the harmonic series of the new, higher note, but the mix is sparser and weighted to the low end), so it sounds purer and more flute-like.
</Answer>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="check">
Why can a trumpet, with valves, play a chromatic scale, while a bugle can only play the notes of a major chord? And why is the bugle's lowest playable note usually not the bottom of its chord?

<Answer>
A bugle is one fixed tube; the player can only choose among its modes by changing lip tension, and modes 2 to 6 of a harmonic series form a major chord (with the octave doubled). A trumpet's three valves can add tubing in seven combinations, giving seven different tube lengths, each with its own series of modes, which together fill in every semitone. The lowest mode of a brass instrument is out of place (the bell and mouthpiece pull the other modes into line, not that one) and hard to play, so the usable series starts on mode 2.
</Answer>
</Callout>

## What makes an instrument recognizable

Put the pieces together and you can see why timbre (chapter 1.2's "recipe of harmonics") is only the beginning of an instrument's identity.

- **The steady spectrum** comes from the exciter (a bow's sawtooth, a reed's pulses, a pluck's triangle) filtered by the resonator's modes and the radiator's resonances.
- **The spectrum changes with register.** A body's resonances stay put while the notes move, so each note has its harmonics boosted differently, just as each vowel's formants stay put when you sing higher. A clarinet's low notes are hollow, its middle notes clearer, its top notes shrill. This is why a single "clarinet waveform" played across the whole keyboard sounds fake.
- **The spectrum changes with loudness.** Harder bowing, blowing, or striking puts more energy into the upper harmonics, so loud notes are brighter, not just louder.
- **The beginning and end** are as distinctive as the middle. The <Term id="attack-transient">attack transients</Term> of chapter 1.3 come straight from the physics: the bow's scratchy start before Helmholtz motion settles in; the chiff of a flute's jet finding its rhythm; the clang of a hammer before the high modes die; the way a plucked or struck note decays, with high modes fading first. Bowed and blown notes can swell and hold; struck and plucked ones can only fade.
- **Inharmonicity** gives struck metal, stiff strings, and drums their own colors: the clang of a bell, the shimmer of a gamelan, the slight metallic edge of a piano's top octave.

This is also what physical-modeling synthesis (chapter 4.5) tries to capture: instead of copying a spectrum, it simulates the exciter, the resonator, and the feedback between them, and lets all these behaviors fall out on their own.

## Classifying instruments

In 1914 the musicologists Erich von Hornbostel and Curt Sachs proposed a way to classify every instrument in the world, from every culture, without relying on Western orchestral categories ("strings, woodwinds, brass, percussion") that don't fit most of the world's instruments. Their principle is exactly this chapter's: **classify by what vibrates.**

<DataTable title="The Hornbostel–Sachs classification" caption="The top level of the scheme, with the fifth class added later. Each class divides further (for example, chordophones into zithers, lutes, harps, and lyres; aerophones by whether the air is set vibrating by an edge, a reed, or the lips).">

| Class | What vibrates | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Idiophones** | The body of the instrument itself | Bells, gongs, xylophones, marimbas, cymbals, rattles, the mbira, the triangle |
| **Membranophones** | A stretched membrane | Drums of all kinds: timpani, tabla, djembe, snare drum, kazoo |
| **Chordophones** | A stretched string | Violin, guitar, piano, harp, sitar, koto, oud |
| **Aerophones** | A column or jet of air | Flute, clarinet, trumpet, organ, bagpipes, didgeridoo, harmonica |
| **Electrophones** | Electronic circuits (added later, by Sachs in 1940) | Theremin, synthesizer, electric organ |

</DataTable>

The scheme has quirks. A piano is a struck chordophone, though Western orchestras seat it with percussion. A kazoo is a membranophone: the player hums, and a membrane buzzes. The harmonica and accordion are aerophones built around free metal reeds. And the electric guitar is arguably a chordophone with an electronic radiator. But the core idea, that an instrument's identity starts with its resonator, has held up for over a century.
