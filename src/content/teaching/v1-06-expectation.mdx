## The question

A pattern of air-pressure changes, lasting three minutes, makes a grown adult cry in a car. Nothing in it refers to anything: no words (or none that explain the tears), no pictures, no story. Why does patterned sound move people at all?

There's no single answer, and this chapter will end with a list of several mechanisms. But one idea runs through most current research, and it ties together everything Volume I has built so far:

> **Listening is predicting.** Your brain doesn't wait for the music to happen. It continually guesses what comes next, and much of what music makes you feel comes from those guesses being confirmed, delayed, or overturned.

To predict, you need three things, and they give this chapter its shape:

1. **Memory**, to hold what just happened (and what usually happens).
2. **Attention**, to decide what to follow and when to listen hardest.
3. **A model** of how music tends to go, learned from years of exposure.

Then we'll see how the guesses turn into feelings, and what else, besides prediction, makes music emotional.

## Listening happens in time

A painting is all there at once. A piece of music never is. At any instant you hear one thin slice of it. Everything else is either memory or anticipation. So the first question is how much of the past a listener actually has.

### Three timescales of memory

The composer and theorist Bob Snyder, in *Music and Memory* (2000), organizes musical listening around three memory systems, each with its own timescale:

- **Echoic memory.** A brief, detailed trace of the raw sound, a kind of acoustic afterimage. It's why you can "replay" the last second or two of what someone said even if you weren't paying attention. It holds detail but fades within a few seconds.
- **Short-term memory.** The small amount you can hold in mind at once, a few seconds' worth without rehearsal. Snyder argues this sets the size of the musical *present*: roughly a line of a melody, the length of a sung breath. Notes inside that window are heard as belonging together; notes beyond it have to be remembered rather than heard.
- **Long-term memory.** Everything that lasts: the tune you heard yesterday, how a favorite song goes, and, crucially, your general knowledge of how music in your culture goes.

<DataTable title="Three timescales of musical memory" caption="After Snyder (2000). The boundaries are approximate and vary with the material and the listener.">

| Memory | Holds | For about | What it does in music |
|---|---|---|---|
| Echoic | The raw sound, in detail | Up to a few seconds | Fuses fast events into notes and tone colors; lets you catch a sound you didn't attend to |
| Short-term | A few items or chunks | A few seconds (longer if rehearsed) | The "present": a melodic line, a rhythmic figure, a bar or two |
| Long-term | Pieces, styles, and patterns | A lifetime | Recognizing a tune; knowing what usually comes next; following form over minutes |

</DataTable>

### Chunking

Short-term memory holds only a handful of items. George Miller's famous estimate was "seven, plus or minus two"; later work (Cowan, 2001) suggests nearer four. That sounds hopeless for music, where a single line of melody can contain a dozen notes.

The way out is **chunking**: grouping several items into one unit you already know. A phone number is ten digits, but you remember it as three chunks. A musician hearing a familiar rhythm, a chord pattern, or a scrap of tune holds it as one chunk, not as its separate notes. This is one reason experienced listeners can follow more complex music than beginners: their long-term knowledge supplies bigger chunks, so the same short-term memory holds more music.

The grouping of chapter 1.4 is the raw material here. Notes that form one <Term id="auditory-stream">auditory stream</Term>, close in time and pitch, are also the notes that get chunked together.

### Why music repeats

Music repeats itself far more than speech or prose does. A song's catchiest section returns three or four times; a riff repeats for minutes; a classical theme comes back at the end. Elizabeth Hellmuth Margulis, in *On Repeat* (2014), argues that repetition is one of the things that *makes* sound feel like music.

Her most striking evidence is an illusion discovered by Diana Deutsch. Take a short spoken phrase and loop it. After a few repetitions, most listeners start to hear the speaking voice as *singing*: the pitches of the speech seem to settle into a little tune, and the illusion persists when the phrase is put back into the full sentence. Nothing about the sound changed. Repetition changed how it was heard: from "what is she saying?" to "how does it go?"

Repetition does work for memory, too. You can't predict what you can't remember, and a pattern heard four times is a pattern you can hold, chunk, and anticipate. A pop hook is built to be learned in one listening.

## Attention

Chapter 1.4 showed the brain sorting one tangled waveform into separate streams. Attention is the next step: choosing which stream to follow. And attention is limited.

### What you can follow at once

You can easily follow one melodic line, and with effort two. Beyond that, it gets hard. In a classic study, David Huron (1989) asked musicians to count how many voices were sounding at each moment in polyphonic organ music, where every voice had the same tone color. They were accurate for one, two, and three voices; with four or more, they consistently underestimated. If you can't even count the voices, you certainly can't follow them all.

Composers seem to know this. Huron found that in Bach's many-voiced keyboard works, which can have four or five independent lines, the texture usually thins out or makes one line stand out, rather than demanding that the listener follow every part at once. Arrangers and mixing engineers face the same limit: a mix may contain thirty tracks, but a listener follows two or three, and the rest is heard as background.

<Callout kind="verify" title="voice counting">
Check Huron (1989), "Voice denumerability in polyphonic music of homogeneous timbres," for the exact accuracy figures and stimuli, and whether the Bach observation is from that paper or a later one.
</Callout>

### Attention in time

Attention also has a *rhythm*. The psychologist Mari Riess Jones proposed that attention isn't spread evenly over time. When you hear a regular beat, your attention starts to pulse with it, peaking when the next beat is due. Events that arrive on the beat are perceived more accurately than identical events slightly early or late. This **dynamic attending** theory (Jones & Boltz, 1989; Large & Jones, 1999) links attention directly to the beat: the beat is, among other things, a schedule for when to listen hardest.

That makes the beat a prediction too: a guess about *when*, separate from the guess about *what*. The beat chapter (2.2) builds on this.

## Learning the statistics of music

Where do musical predictions come from? Mostly from exposure. Nobody teaches a child that a melody tends to move by small steps, or that a dominant-seventh chord tends to resolve. They absorb it, the way they absorb the sound patterns of their native language, by hearing thousands of examples.

### What the experiment shows

This is a scaled-down version of an experiment by Jenny Saffran and colleagues. In 1996, Saffran, Aslin, and Newport played 8-month-old infants a two-minute stream of nonsense syllables built the same way ("bidakupadotigolabubidaku..."), then tested whether they could tell the stream's "words" from "part-words" that straddled a boundary. They could. The only clue was the **transitional probability**: how likely one syllable is to follow another. Inside a word it's high; across a boundary it's low, because any word can follow any other. In 1999 the same group (Saffran, Johnson, Aslin & Newport) showed that infants and adults do the same with sequences of *tones*, which is what this lab imitates. Their tone words, too, were made from notes chosen to have no musical meaning.

This ability, picking up the statistical regularities of what you hear without trying and without knowing you're doing it, is called **statistical learning**. It's one of the strongest candidates for how listeners acquire a musical style: by unconsciously tallying which notes, intervals, rhythms, and chords tend to follow which.

Saffran's tone words were built the same way, which makes the task harder and the result more impressive.

### Surprise, measured

If listeners learn probabilities, then each note has a probability given everything before it, and we can measure how surprising it is. Information theory offers a natural measure, called **surprisal** (or information content): minus the base-2 logarithm of the note's probability, in *bits*.

- A note that was 50% likely carries 1 bit of surprisal.
- 25% likely: 2 bits. 12.5%: 3 bits. Each extra bit means half as likely.
- A near-certain note carries almost 0 bits; a 1-in-100 note carries about 6.6.

The psychologist and computer scientist Marcus Pearce built a model called **IDyOM** (Information Dynamics of Music) that learns note-to-note probabilities from a large collection of melodies, then reports the surprisal of every note in a new one. Its surprisal values track listeners' ratings of how expected each note is, and track brain responses to unexpected notes, better than rule-based theories of melodic expectation (Pearce et al., 2010; Pearce, 2018). A model trained on Western folk songs makes Western-sounding predictions; trained on another tradition's music, it predicts that tradition's habits. Expectations are learned, and they're culture-specific.

The lab below has a tiny cousin of IDyOM. It learned its sense of style from the other tunes in this lab (a few hundred notes, where IDyOM uses thousands of melodies), using three things: how big the last move was, how often each note name appears, and a pull back toward the middle of the tune's range. That's crude, but it's enough to play against.

## Three kinds of expectation

Here's a puzzle. Haydn's Symphony No. 94 is nicknamed the "Surprise." In its slow movement, a gentle tune is played quietly, then repeated even more quietly, and at the end of the repeat the whole orchestra hits a loud chord. Audiences in 1792 jumped. But people who know the piece well still feel a jolt, every time. How can something you *know* is coming still surprise you?

<ListeningCue track="haydn-surprise" start="0:00" end="0:45">
The quiet tune, its even quieter repeat, and the chord (around 0:30, an estimate). Listen once knowing it's coming. Does it still jump out?
</ListeningCue>

Following the psychologist Jamshed Bharucha, Huron distinguishes several sources of expectation, which run side by side:

- **Schematic expectations** come from your knowledge of a *style* in general: what usually happens in melodies, chords, and rhythms of the music you grew up with. They're automatic and hard to switch off.
- **Veridical expectations** come from memory of *this particular piece*. If you know the Haydn, your veridical memory knows the chord is coming.
- **Dynamic expectations** are learned *during* the current hearing: the piece's own patterns, picked up as it goes. When a tune repeats its first line, dynamic expectations predict the repeat even if you've never heard the tune before.

(Huron adds a fourth, conscious expectations: what you explicitly think will happen.)

The Haydn chord violates the schematic expectation (quiet, gentle tunes don't usually end in a bang) even while the veridical one predicts it. The schematic system keeps reacting as if it didn't know, which is why the surprise survives familiarity. Huron (following Wittgenstein's version of the puzzle) argues this is also why a well-known piece can still give you a thrill at the same place each time.

## From prediction to feeling

So far: listeners predict, from statistics learned over a lifetime and patterns picked up as the piece goes. Why should predicting feel like anything?

### The brain as a prediction machine

The broadest version of the answer is the theory of **predictive processing** (also called predictive coding). It holds that the brain is constantly generating predictions about incoming sensation and mainly processes the *difference*, the prediction error. Errors drive learning (update the model) and attention (look here, something unexpected). Applied to music, it suggests why both confirmation and surprise matter, and why the sweet spot lies between total predictability and chaos. The groove chapter (2.5) uses the same idea to explain why some rhythmic surprise feels good.

### ITPRA

Huron's book *Sweet Anticipation* (2006) gives a more detailed psychological account, in five parts. He calls it **ITPRA**, after the five responses an expected event produces, two before and three after:

<ItpraTimeline />

- **Imagination.** Before an event, you imagine possible outcomes and feel a little of how each would feel. This is what motivates you to prepare.
- **Tension.** As the moment approaches, arousal and attention rise, so you're ready to respond. The more uncertain the outcome, the more tension.
- **Prediction response.** Right after the event, a quick reward if you predicted it correctly, and a penalty if you didn't, *regardless of whether the outcome is good or bad*. The brain likes being right.
- **Reaction response.** A fast, automatic response to the event itself: a startle at a sudden loud chord, before you've had time to think.
- **Appraisal response.** A slower, conscious assessment: was that good or bad? A loud chord in a concert hall is harmless, so the appraisal is neutral or pleasant.

Two consequences explain a lot of music.

**The prediction effect.** Because correct predictions are rewarding in themselves, familiar, predictable music feels good, and Huron argues listeners misattribute that pleasure to the music ("I love this song") when part of it is the pleasure of having predicted it. This helps explain why people like the music of their own culture, and why a song often grows on you.

**Contrastive valence.** When a negative fast response (a startle, a wrong prediction) is followed by a positive appraisal ("oh, it's fine, that's lovely"), the contrast makes the final feeling *more* positive than if nothing surprising had happened. Relief after a scare feels better than calm. Huron argues that much musical pleasure works this way: a moment of surprise, quickly reappraised as safe.

### Surprise and pleasure

If prediction errors drive feeling, is more surprise better? Not quite. Recent work suggests pleasure depends on surprise *and* uncertainty together. Cheung and colleagues (2019) used a statistical model to rate 80,000 chords from popular songs for how surprising each was and how uncertain the listener's prediction was just before it. Listeners liked a chord most when an uncertain context was resolved by an expected chord, or when a confident prediction was overturned by a surprising one. Both very high and very low surprise were less pleasant than the middle. That's the same "sweet spot" shape found for rhythmic surprise in groove.

Also see Gold et al. (2019) for a related inverted-U.
</Callout>

## Emotion: more than expectation

Expectation explains a lot, but not everything. A sad song played to someone who has never heard anything like it can still sound sad. A sudden loud bang startles a newborn. A song from the summer you fell in love brings back the summer.

### Perceived versus felt

First, a distinction. Music can *express* an emotion that you recognize ("this sounds sad") without making you *feel* it, and it can make you feel something it doesn't express (a sad song you find beautiful and comforting). Researchers separate **perceived** emotion from **felt** emotion (Gabrielsson, 2002), and they often diverge. Perceived emotion is fairly consistent across listeners, and partly across cultures; felt emotion varies a lot more.

Much of perceived emotion comes from cues music shares with the human voice: sad speech and sad music are both slow, quiet, low, and dark in timbre; happy speech and happy music are faster, louder, higher, and brighter (Juslin & Laukka, 2003). You hear the sadness the way you'd hear it in a voice.

### Several routes to feeling

Patrik Juslin and colleagues proposed that music induces emotion through a set of distinct mechanisms, each with its own brain systems, speed, and dependence on learning. The list has grown over the years; the current version goes by the acronym **BRECVEMA**:

<DataTable title="How music can induce emotion" caption="After Juslin (2013) and Juslin & Västfjäll (2008). Several mechanisms often act at once, and they can pull in different directions.">

| Mechanism | What happens | Musical example |
|---|---|---|
| **B**rain stem reflex | An automatic alarm response to sudden, loud, or harsh sound | The Haydn chord; a sudden crash after silence |
| **R**hythmic locking | Your body falls into step with the beat, and arousal follows it | Wanting to move to a fast dance track |
| **E**valuative conditioning | A piece becomes linked to whatever it was paired with | A song you always heard at happy family gatherings |
| **C**ontagion | You "catch" the emotion expressed, as you would from a voice | A sobbing violin line makes you feel sad |
| **V**isual imagery | The music evokes images you respond to | A slow swell that conjures a landscape |
| **E**pisodic memory | It brings back a specific memory | "Our song" |
| **M**usical expectancy | Predictions are confirmed, delayed, or overturned | Everything in this chapter so far |
| **A**esthetic judgment | Appreciating skill, beauty, or originality | Being moved by a performance as a performance |

</DataTable>

The point of the list is that "music is emotional" isn't one fact. A film score can work through contagion and imagery, a dance track through rhythmic locking and the brain stem, a song from your past through episodic memory, all at the same time.

### Chills

The most studied strong response to music is **frisson**, or musical chills: goosebumps or a shiver down the spine at a particular moment. It's useful to researchers because it's a physical, timeable event.

John Sloboda (1991) asked listeners to name passages that gave them chills, tears, or a racing heart, and then looked at what happened in the scores at those moments. Chills clustered at new or unexpected harmonies, sudden changes in loudness or texture, and melodic *appoggiaturas*: notes that clash with the harmony on a strong beat and then resolve by step. Later studies found similar triggers: entrances of a new voice or instrument, a sudden swell, a return after a build (Grewe et al., 2007).

</ListeningCue>

Two brain-imaging studies are often quoted here. Blood and Zatorre (2001) scanned listeners while they heard music that, for them personally, reliably gave chills, and found activity in brain regions involved in reward, the same systems that respond to food and sex. Salimpoor and colleagues (2011) went further, measuring dopamine release: it rose in one region (the caudate) during the *anticipation* of a peak moment, and in another (the nucleus accumbens) at the peak itself. That fits ITPRA nicely: one reward for the approach, another for the arrival.

Some honest limits:

- These studies are small (around ten people each), and participants chose their own music.
- Chills are idiosyncratic. Many people rarely or never get them, and the same passage works for one listener and not another.
- A small minority of people find music almost entirely unrewarding while enjoying other rewards normally, a condition called specific musical anhedonia (Mas-Herrero et al., 2014). Music's link to reward isn't automatic.
- "The brain's reward system lights up" is a description, not an explanation. It says pleasure is real pleasure, not what causes it.

## Familiarity, complexity, and boredom

The prediction effect says familiar music is rewarding. So why don't we just play one song forever?

### Mere exposure

Robert Zajonc (1968) showed that people tend to like things more simply because they've encountered them before, even nonsense words and shapes they don't remember seeing. This **mere-exposure effect** holds for music too: in general, liking for an unfamiliar piece rises over the first several hearings (Peretz, Gaudreau & Bonnel, 1998). It's one reason radio repetition works, and one reason a song you disliked at first can grow on you.

### Too much of a good thing

But the rise doesn't go on forever. Play a piece often enough and liking levels off, then falls. You've exhausted it. Part of this is **habituation**: the fading of a response to a stimulus that keeps repeating unchanged. Your brain stops reacting to what it can perfectly predict, because there's nothing to learn from it.

Put these together and you get a classic idea from the psychologist Daniel Berlyne (1971): pleasure is highest at a *moderate* level of complexity or novelty, lower for music that's too simple or too familiar (boring) and lower again for music that's too complex or too strange (confusing). Plotted, it's an upside-down U. Where the peak sits depends on the listener: more experience with a style moves the peak toward more complex music, because what's complex to a newcomer is predictable to an expert.

A 2017 review of the music research (Chmiel & Schubert) found that most studies support this inverted-U shape, though not all, and the result depends on how complexity is measured.

<DataTable title="What expectation predicts about liking" caption="Each row is a tendency, not a law; individual taste and context shift all of them.">

| Situation | What happens to predictions | Likely response |
|---|---|---|
| First hearing of a style you don't know | Almost nothing can be predicted | Confusing; often disliked |
| First hearing of a song in a familiar style | Schematic predictions work; details surprise | Interest, mild pleasure |
| Fifth hearing | Schematic and veridical predictions mostly confirmed | Liking usually rises (mere exposure) |
| Five-hundredth hearing | Everything predicted perfectly | Boredom (habituation), unless something keeps schematic surprises alive |
| An expert hearing complex music in their own style | Rich predictions, some cleverly overturned | Strong pleasure for the expert, confusion for others |

</DataTable>

<Callout kind="check">
Using this chapter, explain why a composer of a long piece might bring back the opening theme near the end, but changed somehow.

<Answer>
Bringing it back lets veridical and dynamic expectations succeed: the listener recognizes it, and the prediction effect rewards that recognition. Changing it keeps some surprise alive, so the moment isn't simply habituated: a familiar thing, slightly overturned, gives both the pleasure of being right and the contrast of a surprise. Form (chapter 5.5) is largely the large-scale management of this balance.
</Answer>

## The architecture of listening in time

Volume I started with vibrations and ends with feelings. In between, the ear analyzes sound into frequencies (chapters 1.2 and 1.3), the brain regroups them into sources and streams (1.4) and judges how they fit together (1.5). This chapter adds time: memory holds what has passed, attention selects what to follow, and a learned statistical model predicts what's next. The feelings come partly from how those predictions fare and partly from other routes that bypass prediction altogether.
