## Part 1: Pitch

### Sound, frequency, and pitch

Sound is vibration. A string, a drumhead, or your vocal cords move back and forth, pushing the air into tiny ripples of higher and lower pressure that travel to your ear.

The **frequency** of a vibration is how many times per second it repeats, measured in **hertz (Hz)**. A string vibrating 440 times per second produces a 440 Hz tone. Human hearing covers roughly 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz, and the top end falls with age. A piano spans about 27.5 Hz (its lowest note) to 4,186 Hz (its highest).

**Pitch** is how high or low a sound *seems*. Higher frequency means higher pitch. But frequency is physics, something you can measure with a microphone, while pitch is perception, something that happens in your head. Usually they track each other closely. Chapter 1.3 covers the interesting cases where they don't.

> **Keep this:** frequency is measured in Hz; pitch is what you hear; higher frequency means higher pitch.

### The keyboard as a map

A piano keyboard is the most useful picture of pitch in Western music, even if you never play one.

- **White keys** are named with the letters **A B C D E F G**, then the pattern repeats. Find C by looking for the white key just to the left of each group of *two* black keys.
- **Black keys** sit between most white keys and take their names from their neighbors. The black key just above C is **C♯** ("C sharp": sharp means one step up). It's also the black key just below D, so it can equally be called **D♭** ("D flat": flat means one step down). C♯ and D♭ are the same key.
- E–F and B–C have no black key between them. They're already neighbors.

Twelve keys up from any note (counting both black and white) you reach a note with the **same letter name**. That distance is an **octave**, and it's special: the higher note has exactly **twice the frequency** of the lower. A note at 220 Hz, the one at 440 Hz, and the one at 880 Hz all sound so alike that they share a name, A. (Chapter 1.2 explains why octaves sound so similar.)

To say *which* A, musicians add an **octave number**. The numbers count up from C, so the C near the middle of the keyboard is **C4**, better known as **middle C** (about 261.6 Hz). The A above it, **A4**, is the 440 Hz note orchestras tune to. The C an octave below middle C is C3, the A an octave above A4 is A5, and so on.

> **Keep this:** note names repeat every octave; an octave doubles the frequency; middle C is C4 and the tuning A is A4 = 440 Hz.

### Semitones and intervals

The distance from one key to the very next (white or black) is a **semitone**, also called a **half step**. Two semitones make a **whole step**. An octave is 12 semitones.

The distance between any two notes is an **interval**, and intervals have names:

| Semitones | Name | Short | A tune that starts with it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | minor second | m2 | the *Jaws* theme |
| 2 | major second | M2 | "Happy Birthday" ("-py *birth*-") |
| 3 | minor third | m3 | "Greensleeves" |
| 4 | major third | M3 | "When the Saints Go Marching In" |
| 5 | perfect fourth | P4 | "Here Comes the Bride" |
| 6 | tritone | TT | "Maria" (*West Side Story*) |
| 7 | perfect fifth | P5 | "Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star" |
| 8 | minor sixth | m6 | the leap in "The Entertainer" |
| 9 | major sixth | M6 | "My Bonnie Lies Over the Ocean" |
| 10 | minor seventh | m7 | "Somewhere" (*West Side Story*) |
| 11 | major seventh | M7 | the leap in "Take On Me" |
| 12 | octave | P8 | "Somewhere Over the Rainbow" |

The names look odd at first. Why is a "third" four semitones? Because interval names count **letter names**, not keys. C to E spans three letters (C, D, E), so it's a third, whatever happens with the black keys in between. Seconds, thirds, sixths, and sevenths each come in two sizes, **minor** (smaller) and **major** (larger). Fourths, fifths, and octaves are called **perfect**, and the **tritone** sits in the middle of the octave. Larger intervals continue the count: a **tenth** is a third plus an octave.

</Callout>

> **Keep this:** a semitone is one key; intervals are named by counting letters (third, fifth…) with a quality (major, minor, perfect).

### Ratios, equal temperament, and cents

Here's a fact that sounds technical but explains a lot: **the ear hears frequency ratios, not differences.** An octave always doubles the frequency, whether it's 110 → 220 Hz or 1,000 → 2,000 Hz, even though the second gap in Hz is ten times bigger. The same goes for every interval. A fifth is always (about) a **3:2** ratio, a fourth about **4:3**, and a major third about **5:4**, at any pitch. That's why a tune sounds like the same tune in any key.

A modern keyboard is tuned in **equal temperament**: the octave is divided into 12 *exactly equal* semitones, each one multiplying the frequency by the same amount (about 1.0595, the twelfth root of 2). The result is that most intervals come out very close to the simple ratios but not exactly on them. An equal-tempered fifth is 1.498, not 1.500. Chapter 1.5 shows why those small differences matter.

For measuring small differences like that, musicians use **cents**: 100 cents to an equal-tempered semitone, so 1,200 cents to an octave. Most people start to notice a mistuning somewhere around 5–10 cents. The keyboard above shows each interval's size in cents and its frequency ratio.

> **Keep this:** intervals are ratios (octave 2:1, fifth ≈ 3:2); equal temperament makes all 12 semitones identical; a semitone is 100 cents.

### Register

**Register** just means how high or low something sits in the overall range. "The cellos in their low register" or "a melody in the upper register." The same note name in a different octave is the same note *in a different register*. That matters more than you might expect, as chapter 1.5 shows.

### Chords

A **chord** is three or more notes sounding together. The basic chord is the **triad**: three notes stacked in thirds. The bottom note is the **root**, and it gives the chord its name. Above it come the **third** and the **fifth**.

Triads come in two main flavors, and the only difference is the size of that middle third:

- A **major triad** stacks a major third, then a minor third: C–E–G is **C major**.
- A **minor triad** stacks a minor third, then a major third: C–E♭–G is **C minor**.

Both have a perfect fifth from root to top. Western listeners tend to hear major as bright and minor as dark. That association is real, but it's at least partly learned, a theme that comes up throughout the book.

<KeyboardRef mode="triad" />

<Callout kind="try">
1. Click **C4** and switch between **Major** and **Minor**. Only the middle note moves, by one semitone, yet the character changes completely.
2. Build **A minor** (click A3) and then **C major** (click C4). They share two notes, C and E. Chords that share notes sound closely related.
3. Build **E major**. The third is a black key, G♯. Every key on the keyboard can be a root.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="check">
What notes make up G major? D minor?

<Answer>
G major: G–B–D (G up a major third to B, then a minor third to D). D minor: D–F–A (D up a minor third to F, then a major third to A).
</Answer>
</Callout>

> **Keep this:** a triad is root + third + fifth; major has the bigger third on the bottom, minor the smaller.

## Part 2: Time

### Beat

Most music has a **beat**: the steady pulse you tap your foot or nod your head to. Beats are counted: "**1**, 2, 3, 4, **1**, 2, 3, 4." They're the main landmarks of musical time, the grid everything else is placed against.

### Tempo

**Tempo** is how fast the beat goes, measured in **beats per minute (BPM)**. At 60 BPM there's one beat per second. At 120 BPM there are two, each lasting 500 milliseconds. Slow ballads often sit around 60–80 BPM, much pop and hip-hop around 80–120, house music around 120–130, and drum and bass around 170. These are rough ranges with plenty of exceptions.

> **Keep this:** tempo = beats per minute; 60 BPM is one beat per second.

### Meter and bars

Beats fall into repeating groups called **bars** (or **measures**), usually of two, three, or four beats. The first beat of each bar, the **downbeat**, feels strongest: it's where the pattern starts again. The grouping is called the **meter**.

Meter is written as a **time signature**. The most common by far in popular music is **4/4**: four beats per bar, with each beat written as a *quarter note*. The waltz is in **3/4**: three beats per bar, "**1** 2 3, **1** 2 3." (Some meters group beats in more complicated ways. Chapter 2.1 covers them.)

<Metronome controls={['tempo', 'meter']} beatsPerBar={3} bpm={150} />

<Callout kind="try">
Play the metronome in **3** and count along: "**1** 2 3." Switch to **4**: "**1** 2 3 4." Notice how the higher accented click on each downbeat makes you feel the groups. Then put on the waltz below and count along.
</Callout>

<ListeningCue track="strauss-blue-danube">
Skip past the slow, quiet introduction to where the main waltz tune begins, then count "**1** 2 3, **1** 2 3." The accompaniment does the work for you: a low note on 1, then two lighter chords on 2 and 3, the classic "oom-pah-pah."
</ListeningCue>

> **Keep this:** beats group into bars; the downbeat starts each bar; 4/4 = four beats per bar, 3/4 = three.

### Subdivision

Beats can be split into smaller equal parts, called **subdivisions**:

- Split each beat in **two** to get **eighth notes**, counted "**1** & **2** & **3** & **4** &."
- Split it in **four** to get **sixteenth notes**, counted "**1** e & a **2** e & a…"
- Split it in **three** to get **triplets**, counted "**1** trip-let **2** trip-let…"

(The names come from notation. In 4/4 the beat is a quarter note, so half a beat is an eighth note and a quarter of a beat is a sixteenth.)

<Metronome controls={['tempo', 'subdivision']} subdivision={2} bpm={90} />

<Callout kind="try">
At 90 BPM, switch between **Eighths** and **Triplets** and count aloud along with the lights. Eighths feel square and even; triplets feel rolling and lilting. Then try **Sixteenths**. That busy "1 e & a" grid is what most dance and hip-hop beats are built on, and it's where chapter 2.5 starts.
</Callout>

> **Keep this:** subdivisions split the beat into 2 (eighths), 4 (sixteenths), or 3 (triplets).

**Misconceptions to watch for:**

- "An octave is eight notes." It spans eight letter names counted inclusively (C to C), but it's twelve semitones.
