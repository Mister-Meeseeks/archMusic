## The question

Play G7 on a piano and stop. Almost anyone raised on Western music, trained or not, feels that something is missing. Play C next and the feeling goes away. Nothing about G7 is broken. It isn't especially rough (chapter 1.5 showed that a G7 voiced well is milder than plenty of chords that sound perfectly settled). Yet it *points*.

The idea that chords have jobs in a key, that some are home, some are departures, and some are the road back, is called **harmonic function**. It's the central idea of Western harmony from about 1650 to 1900, it still runs most film music, hymns, jazz standards, and a good share of pop, and it's what most people mean when they say a song "resolves."

This chapter asks three questions:

1. **What are the jobs?** Which chords are stable, which push, and why.
2. **Where does the push come from?** Part acoustics, part voice leading, and a lot of habit.
3. **How universal is it?** Rock, blues, and the modern pop loop bend the rules, and the experimental evidence suggests function is something listeners learn.

## Home, away, and the pull back

### Three families

Chapter 3.5 built seven <Term id="diatonic-chords">chords from the C major scale</Term> and named them with <Term id="roman-numerals">Roman numerals</Term>: I, ii, iii, IV, V, vi, vii°. In music of the common practice they don't behave like seven equals. They fall into three families:

- **Tonic.** I is home: the chord a piece starts from and ends on. vi (and, more weakly, iii) can stand in for it, because they share two of its three notes. A minor (A C E) contains C and E; so does C major (C E G).
- **Dominant.** V (and V7, and vii°) is the chord of maximum pull toward home. It's the road back.
- **Predominant.** IV and ii are the chords that lead *to* the dominant. They move away from home without yet pulling back. (The older name for IV is the *subdominant*, and you'll see "subdominant function" used to mean the whole family.)

The most common shape of a phrase runs through all three in order:

**Tonic → Predominant → Dominant → Tonic**

Call it the **phrase model**: away from home, through a gathering pull, back. I–IV–V–I is the plainest version; I–vi–ii–V–I is the jazz-standard and doo-wop version; a Bach chorale phrase or a Mozart theme is usually an elaboration of the same plan.

### Why the dominant pulls

There are three overlapping explanations. None is enough on its own.

**1. The leading tone.** G7 contains B, the seventh note of the scale, a semitone below C. A note a semitone from the tonic, inside the chord that most often precedes the tonic, has a strong tendency to rise into it. That note is called the **leading tone**. Melodies everywhere tend to settle by step onto stable notes (chapter 3.3), and the leading tone is the closest neighbor home has.

**2. The tritone squeezes inward.** G7 also contains F. B and F are a <Term id="interval">tritone</Term> apart, the interval that splits the octave exactly in half and the only one in the major scale that is neither a perfect nor a major/minor third-or-sixth relative of the tonic triad. In the move to C, B rises a semitone to C and F falls a semitone to E. Both notes of the most unstable interval in the key resolve by the smallest possible step into the two most stable notes of the tonic chord. Chapter 3.5's voice-leading lab shows the same move: two voices, two semitones.

**3. The bass falls a fifth.** G down to C is a fifth (or up a fourth). Root motion by a falling fifth is the most common root motion in tonal music by a wide margin. The eighteenth-century theorist Jean-Philippe Rameau built a whole theory on it. He noted that G is the third harmonic of C (chapter 1.2), so moving from G to C is, in his picture, returning to the note that generates the other. That's a historical explanation, not a proven one, but the statistic it explains is real.

And over all three: **habit.** Dominant-to-tonic is the most common way to end a phrase in several centuries of music. Your expectations were trained on that history (chapter 1.6), and expectation is what turns a chord into a question.

## Cadences: the punctuation of phrases

A **cadence** is a conventional chord move that ends a phrase. Chapter 5.4 introduced the idea as punctuation; here is the full set. The names come from classical theory, but every one of them is everywhere in popular music.

<DataTable title="Cadences" caption="Strength is about how final the ending feels, not how loud or rough it is. In the strongest authentic cadence, both chords are in root position and the melody lands on the tonic note.">

| Cadence | Chords (C major) | Punctuation | Where you hear it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Authentic** | V(7) → I (G7 → C) | Full stop | The last bars of almost any classical piece; the ends of hymns and jazz standards |
| **Half** | Ends on V (… → G) | Comma or question mark | The middle of a two-phrase tune, where the first phrase "asks" and the second "answers" |
| **Plagal** | IV → I (F → C) | A softer full stop, an "Amen" | The "Amen" at the end of hymns; very common in rock and gospel |
| **Deceptive** | V → vi (G → Am) | A sidestep: the period becomes a comma | Delaying an ending; the expected home is swapped for a near neighbor |
| **♭VII → I** (rock) | B♭ → C | A full stop without a dominant | The endings of rock songs (see below) |

</DataTable>

The half cadence makes the most sense alongside chapter 3.3's antecedent and consequent phrases: a first phrase that ends on V sounds like a question, and a second phrase that ends V–I answers it. Most of the tunes you know with a two-part "question and answer" shape work this way.

## The circle of fifths

Arrange the twelve notes so that each is a fifth above the last: C, G, D, A, E, B, F♯, D♭, A♭, E♭, B♭, F, and back to C. That loop is the **circle of fifths**. It does two jobs.

**Keys.** Neighboring notes on the circle are neighboring keys. C major and G major share six of their seven notes (G major swaps F for F♯). Each step clockwise adds a sharp; each step counterclockwise adds a flat. The seven roots of C major's chords sit together on one stretch of the circle, from F to B. Moving to a key next door on the circle is easy and smooth; moving to the far side is a jolt.

**Root motion.** Progressions in the common practice mostly move their roots *counterclockwise*, one step at a time: a falling fifth. iii → vi → ii → V → I is the whole C major stretch of the circle walked backwards, ending at home. The jazz turnaround (I–vi–ii–V), the "Autumn Leaves" sequence, and long Baroque sequences (chapter 3.3) are all this same walk. Because each chord is the dominant of the next, or close to it, the chain builds momentum: every arrival is also a departure.

In the common lead-sheet key the chords run Am – Dm – G – C – F – B(ø) – E – Am: every root falls a fifth, walking counterclockwise all the way round, with one diminished fifth (F to B) to stay inside the key. Listen to how the melody steps down a scale while the harmony falls in fifths underneath it.
</ListeningCue>

## Borrowing the pull: secondary dominants

If G7 pulls to C because it's C's dominant, any major chord can be given its own dominant. D7 is the dominant of G. E7 is the dominant of A minor. Slip D7 in before G and, for a moment, G sounds like a temporary home that you are about to arrive at.

These borrowed dominants are **secondary dominants**, written V/V ("five of five": D7 in C), V/vi (E7), V/ii (A7), and so on. They bring in notes from outside the key (D7's F♯, E7's G♯), and each one has its own leading tone into its target. Making a chord other than I feel briefly like home this way is called **tonicization**.

Chain several and you get the ragtime and barbershop progression E7 → A7 → D7 → G7 → C (try the **Ragtime chain** preset): each chord is the dominant of the next, so the pull never lets up until the final C.

<ListeningCue track="beatles-yesterday" start="0:00" end="0:20">

That's where the melody's "all my troubles seemed so far away" begins to sag. Then B♭ → C7 → F brings it home.
</ListeningCue>

## Changing key: modulation

Tonicization is a visit. **Modulation** is a move: the music settles in a new key, with its own cadences, long enough that the old tonic stops feeling like home.

The smoothest way to modulate uses a **pivot chord**, a chord that belongs to both keys. A minor is vi in C major and ii in G major. So C – Am – D7 – G starts in C, reinterprets A minor as G's predominant, and lands on G through G's own dominant (D7). The ear is moved before it notices. Neighboring keys on the circle share most of their chords, which is why moves to the next key along are the most common modulations. The dominant key (C to G) is the classic move, and the next-door minor key (C major to A minor, which uses the same seven notes) is another.

Modulation is the engine of large forms. Chapter 5.5 shows how the largest classical forms are built on a move to a second key and back; chapter 5.4 shows the pop version, a final shift up a step to lift a song's ending.

<Callout kind="check">
In the lab, build C – Am – V/V (D7) – V (G) and stop. Does it sound like it ended at home, or away from home? What would you need to add to make G sound like the *new* home rather than C's dominant?

<Answer>
It usually sounds like a half cadence in C: ending on G after D7 points at G, but G is still heard as C's dominant. To make G the new home it needs to be confirmed: a full cadence in G (for example Am – D7 – G again, or C – D7 – G with the new key's characteristic F♯ repeated), more time spent in G, and a melody that settles on G. One arrival is a tonicization; a confirmed one, several bars long, is a modulation. There's no exact line between them.
</Answer>
</Callout>

## How fast the chords change

The rate at which chords change is the **harmonic rhythm**. It's a separate dial from tempo. A hymn may change chord on every beat; a funk tune may sit on one chord for a minute. A common way to drive into a cadence is to speed the harmonic rhythm up: one chord per bar, then two, then one per beat, then a long tonic. The slowing that follows the arrival is part of what makes the arrival feel final.

## Function in popular music

Everything so far is the common-practice system. Popular music grew from it, but also from the blues, folk, gospel, and modal traditions that never followed it strictly. Several habits stand out.

### The blues: sevenths that stay put

In classical harmony a dominant seventh chord is unstable; it exists to resolve. In the blues, *every* chord can be a dominant seventh: I7, IV7, and V7. The C7 at the start of a blues in C is home, not a pointer to F. The minor seventh in the chord is part of the music's color, closer to the "blue" notes of the melody than to a question needing an answer. The I–IV–V skeleton is still there, and the turn from V back to I still sounds like a return, but the dominant seventh has been redefined.

</ListeningCue>

### Rock: IV before V, and the ♭VII

Trevor de Clercq and David Temperley analyzed the chords of 100 rock songs from *Rolling Stone*'s list of the greatest of all time. Their corpus departs from the common-practice picture in several ways. IV is more common than V. Moves from IV to I are about as common as V to I. Moves from V to IV, which classical theory treats as "backwards," are routine. And the chord on the flattened seventh degree, **♭VII** (B♭ in C), is a staple.

♭VII isn't in the C major scale. It comes from the Mixolydian <Term id="mode">mode</Term> (chapter 3.4), the major scale with a lowered seventh, or from C minor. A chord brought in from a parallel mode or key while staying in the original key is a **borrowed chord**, and the practice is called **modal mixture**. The ♭VII → IV → I progression is rock's own cadence. Every root falls a fourth (B♭ → F → C), which is the same as rising a fifth: on the circle it walks *clockwise* into home, the mirror image of the classical falling fifths. (The plagal IV → I makes the same clockwise step.) It reaches home without a dominant chord or a leading tone at all. Compare the **I ♭VII IV I** and **Round the circle** presets in the lab and watch which way the arrows turn.

</ListeningCue>

### The loop

From the 1990s onward, a large share of pop is built on a **chord loop**: a four-chord pattern that repeats for most of the song, I–V–vi–IV being the most famous. A loop has no first or last chord, so the phrase model's beginning and end disappear. The V in I–V–vi–IV usually goes to vi, not I; the IV goes back to I, plagally. Some analysts argue that in many loops the chords barely have functions at all: the sense of home is carried by the melody, the bass, and which chord the loop seems to start on, not by a dominant pulling to a tonic. Others hear the old functions still operating in softened form. The same four chords rotated (vi–IV–I–V) can even make A minor sound like home.

Try the **I V vi IV loop** preset. Then build the rotation **vi IV I V** and play it a few times. Where is home?

### Borrowed color

Besides ♭VII, rock and film music often borrow **iv** (F minor in C major) and **♭VI** (A♭). IV turning into iv lowers one note by a semitone (A to A♭), a small, bittersweet darkening that's often described as nostalgic. Try the **Borrowed from minor** preset.

## Is function real?

Is "dominant pulls to tonic" a fact about sound, or a convention? The evidence points mostly to convention, learned very well.

- **People learn it without lessons.** In harmonic priming experiments, a target chord is identified faster, and judged in tune more readily, when the chords before it make it the expected next chord. Listeners with no musical training show the effect too (Bharucha and Stoeckig 1986; many follow-ups by Tillmann, Bigand, and colleagues). Brain-response studies find a distinct response to an out-of-place chord, again in nonmusicians (Koelsch and colleagues, 2000).
- **The expectations match the statistics.** Computer models that do nothing but absorb chord sequences from a large corpus develop expectations very like listeners'. That's consistent with function being a learned summary of what usually comes next: the predictive picture of chapter 1.6.
- **It isn't universal.** Raga, maqam, and gamelan (chapter 3.10) organize pitch around other principles: a drone, characteristic melodic phrases, interlocking cycles. They have no dominant–tonic engine. Even within Western music the blues and rock have rewritten parts of the system.
- **The physics helps, but doesn't decide.** The leading tone's semitone step and the tritone's inward squeeze give the dominant a strong voice-leading pull, and fifth relationships are acoustically close (chapter 1.2). Those properties probably explain why this system rather than some other became the one people learned. They don't make it inevitable.

The honest summary is that function is a *schema*: a set of expectations, built from exposure, partly grounded in acoustics and voice leading, that composers can satisfy, delay, or deny. Chapter 5.4 is about what happens when they do.
