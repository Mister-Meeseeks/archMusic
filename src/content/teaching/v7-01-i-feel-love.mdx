## The record

In the summer of 1977, Donna Summer released a song on the B-side of another single. It was the B-side that caught on, and about two months later the label reissued the record with the sides reversed. "I Feel Love" went to number one in Britain and the top ten in the United States. It sounded like nothing else on the radio.

Most disco of the time was made by large studio bands: a rhythm section, strings, horns, sometimes an orchestra. "I Feel Love" had almost none of that. Apart from Summer's voice and (probably) the kick drum, every sound on it came from a <Term id="synthesizer">synthesizer</Term>, played not by a person's hands but by a <Term id="sequencer">sequencer</Term>, a device that steps through a stored pattern of notes at a fixed tempo. The bass never rushes, never drags, never varies. Over it floats a high, soft, sustained voice.

That pairing, a machine and a human, the exact and the free, is the subject of this case study. It touches nearly every chapter in the book.

## How it was made

### The people and the room

The record was made at Musicland Studios in Munich by three people with distinct roles:

- **Giorgio Moroder**, an Italian producer based in Munich, composed the music.
- **Pete Bellotte**, his English co-producer, wrote most of the words.
- **Donna Summer**, an American singer who had lived in Germany for years, sang it, and shares the writing credit.

The album *I Remember Yesterday* was a concept record that moved through decades of popular style, from 1940s big-band music to Motown. The last track was meant to represent the future, and Moroder made it with machines.

### A borrowed Moog and a pulse on tape

Moroder didn't own the instrument that made it possible. The big modular Moog synthesizer used on the record was borrowed from Eberhard Schoener, a Munich composer, and it came with Schoener's assistant, **Robby Wedel**, who knew how to run it. A modular synthesizer is a cabinet of separate modules (oscillators, filters, envelope generators, a sequencer) joined by patch cables. Nothing about it was simple to operate.

The problem Wedel solved was **synchronization**. The studio recorded onto <Term id="multitrack-recording">multitrack</Term> tape: sixteen parallel tracks on one reel, so each part could be recorded separately and added to the others later. That's called **overdubbing**. But a sequencer runs on its own internal clock. If you record the bass pattern on one pass and a second synthesizer part on another, the two clocks drift apart and the parts slide out of time.

Wedel's fix was to record a steady electronic pulse onto one track of the tape, a <Term id="click-track">sync track</Term>. On every later pass, the tape played that pulse back into the sequencer, and the sequencer stepped forward on each pulse. The tape became the master clock. Every synthesizer part, recorded at a different time, locked to the same grid. Moroder later called this "a revelation."

The machine had another problem: the oscillators of the period drifted out of tune as they warmed up. By one account the parts had to be recorded in short bursts of 20 or 30 seconds, retuning in between.

## Sound: a sawtooth, a filter, and noise

Start with the bass, the sound that opens the record and never stops.

It's a textbook case of <Term id="subtractive-synthesis">subtractive synthesis</Term>. An <Term id="oscillator">oscillator</Term> produces a waveform rich in <Term id="harmonics">harmonics</Term>, such as a sawtooth. A low-pass <Term id="filter">filter</Term> removes the upper harmonics. An <Term id="envelope">envelope</Term> opens the filter at the start of each note and closes it again a fraction of a second later. So every note starts bright and buzzy and immediately darkens: a short, round thump with a bright edge. Because each note is shorter than the gap between notes, the line stays clear and punchy instead of smearing together.

2. Slowly lengthen **Filter decay**. The notes get brighter and longer, and the line turns from a pulse into a buzz. Shorten it again.

4. Compare **Raw saw**: the same oscillator with no filter movement. Almost all of the character is in the filter and its envelope.
</Callout>

The **hi-hat** is synthesized too, and in the simplest possible way. Take **white noise**, a hiss containing every frequency at once with no pitch at all, and give it a very short envelope. A burst of noise a few hundredths of a second long, with the low frequencies filtered out, is enough for the ear to hear a cymbal.

The **kick drum** is the interesting exception. A good kick needs a hard, sudden attack: a click that lasts a few thousandths of a second, then a low thump that falls in pitch. By Moroder's account, the Moog's version came out soft, more "oomph" than punch, so a drummer played the kick on a real drum. Whether that's right is in the table above. Listen for yourself: does the kick sound like the hi-hat's world (electronic, perfectly identical every time) or like a struck drum?

<Callout kind="check">
In the lab, the bass's filter envelope is short. Why would a *long* filter decay be a problem in this particular record, where the bass plays a note every eighth note and an echo fills the gaps between?

<Answer>
The notes would overlap. Each note would still be bright and loud when the next one (or its echo) arrives, and the line would smear into a continuous buzz. The short envelope leaves a little silence after every note, and that silence is what lets you hear sixteen separate pulses per bar. The echo trick depends on it.
</Answer>
</Callout>

## Rhythm: eighth notes plus an echo

### The trick

The bass seems to play a fast, unbroken stream of sixteenth notes, four per beat. According to most accounts, it doesn't. The sequencer plays half that many, one note every eighth note, and a <Term id="delay-effect">delay effect</Term> supplies the rest.

A delay records the sound and plays it back a fixed time later, mixed with the original. Set that time to exactly one sixteenth note at the song's tempo, and each echo lands precisely in the gap after the note that made it. The pattern of eight notes becomes a stream of sixteen. Nobody played half of them.

<EchoLab />

<Callout kind="try">
1. Press **Play** with the echo **Off**. Eight notes per bar, bouncing between a low note and the same note an octave up. Fine, but plain.
2. Choose **A sixteenth**. The gaps fill and the line suddenly *moves*: it sounds twice as fast and much more driving. Watch the outlined notes slot into the gaps.
3. Choose **An eighth**. Now each echo lands exactly on the next note, so it doubles the notes instead of filling the gaps. You're back to eight pulses per bar, just thicker.
4. Choose **A dotted eighth** (three sixteenths). The gaps fill again, but each gap now holds an echo of the note *before last*. Same rhythm, different order of pitches. Switch to the **Root, octave, fifth, seventh** pattern to hear the difference clearly.
5. Turn on **Kick on every beat** and **Off-beat hi-hat**. You now have the rhythmic skeleton of a whole genre of dance music.
</Callout>

### One stream or two?

Here's a puzzle from <a href="../v1-04-scene-analysis/">*Auditory scene analysis*</a>. On the original mix, by most accounts, the notes the sequencer played are on the left channel and their echoes are on the right. So two separate sources, at two places, each play eight notes per bar. Why do we hear one line of sixteen?

Turn on **Notes left, echo right** in the lab and listen on headphones.

<Callout kind="check">
Using the grouping cues, explain why the left and right channels fuse into a single <Term id="auditory-stream">stream</Term> rather than two interleaved ones. What would you change to make them split apart?

<Answer>
Almost every cue says "one source." The notes and echoes have identical timbre (the echo is a copy), identical pitches, and they alternate quickly and evenly: each note sits a sixteenth away from its neighbors, in the same register. Location is the only cue pointing toward two sources, and location is a weaker grouping cue than pitch, timing, and timbre. To make them split, give the echo a different timbre (filter it, as tape echoes naturally do), move it to a different register (an echo an octave up), or slow the tempo so the notes are further apart in time.
</Answer>
</Callout>

### The grid and the body

Underneath the bass sit the drums, in the pattern the lab just built. The kick hits on every beat, *one, two, three, four*: a pattern called **four-on-the-floor**. The hi-hat hits exactly halfway between, on every off-beat. Kick, hat, kick, hat: an even alternation twice as fast as the beat, which pulls the body in two directions at once, down on the beat and up on the off-beat. Ten years later, this exact combination became the foundation of house and techno.

Everything machine-made here is exactly on the grid. There's no <Term id="microtiming">microtiming</Term>, none of the tiny pushes and lags that human players use to make a groove feel alive (<a href="../v2-05-groove/">*Swing, microtiming, and groove*</a>). And yet it grooves hard. Part of the answer is in that chapter: a busy, syncopated-sounding pattern and a strong, unambiguous beat can do the work that microtiming does elsewhere. Part of it is the voice, which doesn't sit on the grid at all. More on that below.

## Pitch: one pattern, moved by a key

### Transposition as harmony

The bass pattern is short: a few notes bouncing between a low note and the note an <Term id="octave">octave</Term> above, with other notes of the chord in between. What makes the song's harmony is not new patterns but the same pattern moved. On the sequencer, pressing a different key on the keyboard <Term id="melodic-transformation">transposed</Term> the whole pattern. Moroder described it simply: press C and you get the pattern; press another key and you get the same pattern, higher.

So the song's chord changes are really one shape, shifted to new <Term id="root">roots</Term>. One transcription (a user-contributed one, so check it) gives the roots as C, E♭, F, and G. In <Term id="roman-numerals">Roman numerals</Term> in the key of C, that's I, ♭III, IV, V. The ♭III, a major chord on E♭, doesn't belong to the C major scale: it comes from C minor. It gives the song a darker, bluesier tint than a plain I–IV–V would.

Try it in the lab: choose different keys under **Key pressed**, or turn on **Change key every two bars**. It's a crude imitation, but you'll hear how much harmonic motion one pattern can make.

### A voice above the machine

Summer's melody works on a completely different timescale. The bass changes pitch sixteen times a bar; the voice holds notes for a beat, two beats, a whole bar. It moves mostly by small steps, in a narrow range, high in her <Term id="register">register</Term>.

And she sings it in <Term id="head-voice">head voice</Term>. Most disco vocals of the time were belted in chest voice, the full, strong register used for speaking. Head voice, higher and lighter, has fewer strong upper harmonics and a breathier, purer sound. On "I Feel Love," it sits far above the bass in pitch and is utterly different in texture: soft, sustained, and with its own small fluctuations in pitch and timing. The machine is rigid; the voice floats. The record sets the two against each other and never settles the contrast.

That contrast also helps the ear. The voice occupies a band of frequencies the bass barely touches, so neither <Term id="masking">masks</Term> the other. And its smooth pitch movements, all harmonics moving together, bind it into one clear source against the machine's clicks and pulses.

## Architecture: six minutes of almost nothing changing

### The form

On paper, the <Term id="musical-form">form</Term> is almost nothing. There's no verse that tells a story and then a contrasting <Term id="song-sections">chorus</Term>. Instead, a handful of short vocal phrases and the title's refrain come and go over a pattern that never stops, with stretches of instrumental in between. The bass is there from the first second to the last.

<SongMap track="donna-summer-i-feel-love" prompt="The section times are a rough skeleton, not a transcription. Use Retime to fix them by ear, and add an event for every key change you hear." />

How does a track with so little change hold attention for six minutes? The tools are the ones from <a href="../v5-04-tension/">*Tension, release, contrast, climax*</a>, used with unusual restraint:

- **Transposition as refresh.** Each shift of the pattern to a new root arrives just as you've stopped noticing the old one. Repetition breeds <Term id="habituation">habituation</Term>; the key shift breaks it, and the pattern seems new again without being new.
- **Adding and removing layers.** Parts enter and drop out: the voice, extra synthesizer lines, effects. The <Term id="texture">texture</Term> thickens and thins while the core never stops.
- **The voice as the variable.** Against a constant machine, every entrance and exit of the voice is an event.

This is a different architecture from the section-based song forms of the *Form* chapter, and much closer to the loop-based forms of the planned chapter *Cyclic and process forms*. It's architecture by layering rather than by sections: what's playing right now matters more than where you are in a plan.

## Production: the mix as composition

In this record, some of the most important musical decisions were made after the playing was over.

The echo is the clearest case. If the accounts are right, the delay that turns eight notes into sixteen was added while mixing, after the bass had been recorded. One of the defining features of the composition, its rhythm, was created at the mixing desk.

Then there's <Term id="stereo-panning">stereo panning</Term>. A stereo recording has two channels, left and right; panning places each sound somewhere between them. By Moroder's account, the original bass was on the left and the delayed copy on the right, so the sixteenth notes flicker from side to side. Years later he said he'd prefer them only a little left and right, not at the extremes. That's a composer revising a rhythm by turning two knobs.

<Callout kind="check">
In the lab, set the echo to a sixteenth and toggle **Notes left, echo right** on and off, on headphones. What does hard panning add, and what might Moroder have disliked about it?

<Answer>
Panned apart, the line gains a flickering, side-to-side motion and a sense of width: the rhythm literally moves around your head. But it can be tiring on headphones, and it makes each channel alone sound thin (each plays only half the notes). Panned moderately, the line holds together as one solid bass while keeping a hint of motion. Notice that the stream stays one stream either way, as the grouping cues predicted.
</Answer>
</Callout>

## Culture: the sound of the future

### "The sound of the future"

The best-known story about the record comes from David Bowie. In 1977 Bowie and Brian Eno were in Berlin, working on the albums that became Bowie's Berlin trilogy. According to Bowie, Eno came running in one day saying he'd heard the sound of the future, put on "I Feel Love," and told Bowie it would change club music for the next fifteen years. Whether or not it happened exactly like that, the prediction was roughly right.

Not everyone was won over. Robert Moog, whose instrument made the record, praised the voice but felt it sounded as if Summer was "fighting the sequencer."

### What it became

The record's descendants run in several directions:

- **Hi-NRG.** In San Francisco, the producer and synthesist Patrick Cowley made a fifteen-minute remix of "I Feel Love" in 1978, adding new synthesizer layers. It circulated among DJs for years before it was officially released. Cowley also worked with the singer Sylvester, whose "You Make Me Feel (Mighty Real)" (1978) pairs a pulsing electronic bass with a soaring falsetto. Their fast, synthesizer-driven disco, rooted in gay clubs, became known as Hi-NRG (high energy).
- **Synth-pop.** British bands of the late 1970s and early 1980s, such as the Human League, built pop songs from sequencers and synthesizers.
- **House and techno.** In Chicago and Detroit in the mid-1980s, producers built tracks from drum machines and sequencers on the same foundation: a four-on-the-floor kick, off-beat hi-hats, and repeating synthesizer patterns. Several pioneers have named "I Feel Love" as an influence.

7. Harmony by transposition: one pattern, pressed on different keys.
8. The voice against the machine: head voice, long notes, free timing; why the two don't mask each other.
9. How the form holds attention with so little change: key shifts against habituation, layers entering and leaving.
10. The mix as composition: the echo and the panning.
11. The lineage, and which parts of the lore are solid.

**Decisions for your rewrite (and for the Atlas format):**

- **Order of the layers.** I went from the physics up to culture, matching the book's volumes. Alternatives: follow the track in time (first second, first minute, the whole), or follow the story of the session (the machine arrives, the pulse, the bass, the voice, the mix). The time-order version might be the most fun to read with the song map open.
- **How much lore.** The making-of story is famous and partly unreliable. You could keep the disagreements table as a feature (a small lesson in how music history is known), cut it to a footnote, or do your own research and settle what you can.
- **Your own transcription.** The strongest version of this chapter would include your transcription of the bass pattern and the key changes, and a retimed song map. Decide whether the chapter shows the actual pattern or only describes it. A short recreation would be our own audio, but the pattern itself is the composition; ask before putting a note-for-note copy in the book.
- **What an Atlas entry is.** This draft is long. A future entry could be shorter, with more table and map and less prose, leaning on chapters once they exist. Decide on a template before writing the second one.

**Misconceptions to watch for:**

- "It's all synthesized." Probably not quite: the kick may be a real drum, and the voice certainly is.
- "The sequencer plays sixteenth notes." By most accounts it plays eighths; the delay supplies the rest.
- "Machine timing can't groove." This record is the counterexample.
- "Two channels means two streams." Grouping is decided mostly by pitch, timing, and timbre.
- "Moroder invented electronic dance music." The record was a turning point, built on earlier electronic music (Kraftwerk, Moroder's own 1970s experiments) and passed on through other people's work: Cowley, the Chicago and Detroit producers.
